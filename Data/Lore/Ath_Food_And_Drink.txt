# ON THE SUBJECT OF EATING IN ATH
### *A Supplementary Account*
*Krad Kraps*

---

*I have eaten in a great many places and I have opinions about all of them. What follows is not a guide to fine dining — there is not, strictly speaking, fine dining on Ath in any form I would recommend to the uninitiated — but rather an honest account of what one actually puts in one's mouth when travelling this continent, and where, and why. I have organised this by location because what Ath eats is, more than almost anywhere else I have visited, a direct expression of where it sits on the map.*

*A note before beginning: there is a category of fruit and prepared food on Ath that exists only in texts. Pre-Fall records describe cultivated orchard varieties — soft-skinned plums, large sweet apples, a citrus fruit grown in the sheltered southern groves — that have not existed in their cultivated form since the population collapse made the orchards untended. What grows now in the places those orchards stood are wild descendants: smaller, more acidic, occasionally unpleasant. A number of wealthier households in Antitheis are attempting to reconstruct the lost varieties through careful cultivation and the guidance of old texts. I tasted one result. It was approximately correct in shape and entirely wrong in flavour, and the cook serving it seemed aware of this and did not ask my opinion.*

*This category of almost-food, the thing that used to exist in better form, is perhaps the most Athian food there is.*

---

## A COASTAL CITY — ESKAR OR VELLMOUTH

The smell of a Gulf Coast town reaches you before the walls do. By the time you are sitting in one of the long open eating-houses that line the dock district, the smell has become so ambient that you stop registering it as fish and start registering it simply as *here*.

The cooking of the Gulf towns is not delicate. It is direct and salt-forward and built around the assumption that the person eating has been doing physical work since before sunrise and will continue doing so after lunch. This is not a criticism.

**Salt-cured Gulf mackerel with broad bean mash** is the dish you will encounter most often, in the most variations, at every price point. The mackerel is cured for several days in brine, producing a firm, intensely flavoured fillet that is served over a coarse mash of broad beans grown in the volcanic-enriched soil of the coastal farms — darker and more mineral-tasting than Interior beans, in a way that takes getting used to and then becomes something you miss when you leave. The quality of the cure is what separates a good establishment from a poor one. A well-cured mackerel has a particular clean salinity. A poorly-cured one announces itself immediately and unpleasantly.

**The shellfish pot** is the second staple — mussels and small reef crabs boiled in seawater with dried kelp and whatever herbs the cook had available that morning. It arrives as a communal pot, served with flat wheat bread for soaking the broth. The bread here is lighter than the Interior rye, the wheat coming in on trade routes, and the eating-house oil used to dress it is rendered from fish fat rather than any plant source. Visitors from elsewhere find this unexpected. I now find plant oil somewhat lacking by comparison.

**Dried kelp strips** are sold at the counter of most coastal establishments rather than served as a dish. Chewy, intensely saline, somewhat leathery in texture, they are the eating of a working population that needs something in its hands between meals. I developed a habit of carrying a small supply while on the road. They travel well.

**The black sauce** — called by a variety of names across the Gulf towns, none of them officially standardised — is a dark, pungent liquid produced by fermenting small baitfish in salt over several months. The result is an intensely flavoured seasoning used in nearly every Gulf preparation, added to stews, rubbed into fish before curing, stirred into bean mash. I mention it separately because it is arguably the defining flavour of Gulf Coast cooking more than any single dish. Inland travellers tend to find it aggressive. I consider it indispensable and purchased a quantity to carry with me.

**Kelp and river-brine pickles** appear as a side to most meals — vegetables pickled in a brine that incorporates dried kelp, giving them a mineral, oceanic undertone that complements the fish preparations well. Each establishment has its own proportion and each regular customer has an opinion about it.

**The wine** arrives from the Interior via the river routes and is served warm in the colder months, which is the correct way to serve it. The Gulf towns also produce a serviceable ale from traded grain, and the waterfront establishments maintain a tradition of something fermented from shellfish that I tried once and will describe only as a singular experience.

## THE PLATEAU — THE NOTCH AND SIMILAR SETTLEMENTS

There are no eating-houses on the Aldare Plateau in the coastal sense. What exists instead is the communal fire, and the understanding that a traveller who arrives in good faith and with something worth trading will be fed.

The feeding is not elaborate. The plateau people do not have the variety of the coast or the accumulated culinary tradition of the Interior towns. What they have is a deep and practical knowledge of what their environment provides and how to make it last. Every preparation I encountered on the plateau could be made with minimal equipment, transported without spoiling, and consumed without ceremony. This is not poverty of imagination. It is competence.

**The hand mutton** is the meal. I say this without qualification. A section of goat or sheep — the plateau herds are primarily goat, with sheep kept more selectively — is boiled in a large pot with salt and dried mountain herbs, brought to the fireside and placed before the company without further preparation. You eat with your hands. There is no plate. The fat renders into the meat during the long boil and the fat is the flavour, more than the salt or the herbs. An outsider's first instinct is that the preparation is too simple to be interesting. This instinct is wrong. I ate hand mutton several times during my time on the plateau and it improved with each occasion, which is the mark of a dish worth understanding.

**Hard goat cheese** is carried everywhere and eaten at any point in the day that hunger presents itself. Dense, sharp with age, wrapped in cloth that has absorbed some of its flavour over time. Some wheels I encountered had been aging for what the families estimated as several years. These were not for eating fresh — they were grated or shaved in small amounts over other preparations, used as seasoning more than as food in itself.

**Dried meat strips** — thin cuts of goat or sheep, salted and hung in the mountain wind for drying. Dark, intensely concentrated in flavour, essentially indefinitely preserved in the plateau conditions. The plateau people carry these as travel food and eat them as snacks, and they form the primary protein available during the moving seasons when the fire is not practical. They take considerable jaw work. I bought a supply before leaving the plateau and rationed them strictly, which was the correct decision.

**Stone-cooked flatbread** is made from traded grain — the plateau communities do not grow their own, the altitude and aridity make it impractical — cooked directly on hearthstones. Unleavened, slightly charred on one face, eaten with cheese or meat fat or simply alone. The simplicity is not a lack. It is a form of honesty.

**The fermented milk drink** is made from goat or sheep milk left to work in a skin bag, producing something mildly alcoholic, thin, and emphatically sour. It does not improve on acquaintance in the way that hand mutton does. I drank it when offered because declining hospitality on the plateau is not advisable, and I will say that after the third occasion I began to find it less objectionable. Whether this constitutes acquired taste or simple resignation I cannot say.

**Mountain herb tea** is the preparation I found most surprising and most worth noting. The plateau people have extensive and specific knowledge of what grows at altitude and what it does to the human body. The line between tea and medicine is not a line they draw. Several preparations I was offered had clear physical effects — one significantly aided sleep on a cold night, one I am fairly certain reduced the swelling in a joint I had injured on the descent. None of this knowledge is written down. It lives in the older women of the plateau communities and passes by demonstration rather than instruction.

## ANTITHEIS — THREE VERSIONS OF THE SAME CITY

Antitheis does not eat as one city. It eats as three or four cities occupying the same space, with the contents of the bowl being one of the clearest indicators of which city you are currently in. I will describe each level honestly.

### THE OUTER RING

The eating-house of the Outer Ring — and I use the term loosely, since most of what passes for eating-houses here is a fire, a pot, and a counter — operates on the principle of sufficient. The goal is to fill the person in front of you well enough that they can work the afternoon.

**Black porridge** is the foundation of the Outer Ring diet. Rye grain cooked down with rendered fat and salt to a thick, dark, nearly solid consistency. Every native of Antitheis who has eaten this since childhood will tell you it is an acquired taste. They are correct, and the acquisition takes longer than they imply. It is, however, filling in a way that few preparations match, and the fat content sustains labour through a cold morning in a way that lighter foods do not.

**Salt fish on rye bread** requires no description. The rye bread of Antitheis is excellent — the Outer Ring bakers have been working the same starter cultures for generations, and the slow fermentation produces a particular sour depth that is one of the genuinely good things about this city. The salt fish on top of it is the same Gulf mackerel available everywhere, at the price point of someone for whom the cure quality is secondary to the quantity.

**Bean pottage** is the evening meal of the Outer Ring in most seasons — dried beans, water, salt, and whatever fat or scrap the cook had access to. Filling. Consistent. Warm. On cold nights in the outer districts this is sufficient virtue.

**Watered ale** is the drink of the Outer Ring by custom and by economic logic. The ale itself is reliable. The water in it keeps the afternoon functional.

### THE MID CITY

The Mid City tavern is where the food becomes genuinely interesting rather than merely sufficient. The trade routes that run through Safarat ensure a variety of goods available to those who can afford the slightly elevated price.

**Fresh Gulf fish, grilled** is the marker of a Mid City establishment — the trade routes move it fast enough in season to serve it fresh, which the Outer Ring does not generally manage. Grilled over hardwood, dressed with the black sauce from the coast, served with wheat bread that is lighter and less sour than the rye of the outer districts.

**Root vegetables with fish sauce seasoning** — the dark fermented Gulf sauce has made its way inland as a condiment and the Mid City cooks use it with more confidence than the Outer Ring, which tends to find it aggressive. The volcanic-soil root vegetables from the coast, traded up through Safarat, are noticeably better in flavour than the Interior varieties.

**Soft cheese with wheat bread** — a mild cow's milk cheese from the Interior farms, younger and less sharp than the plateau goat varieties, eaten with the better wheat bread. This is the Mid City's version of comfort food.

**Preserved fruit compote** appears occasionally as something approaching a luxury side dish. Made from the wild crab apples and small bitter plums that are the reverted descendants of Pre-Fall orchard varieties. The cook has added honey, which helps. The result is not what the texts describe the original preparations to have been, and a well-read Mid City diner knows this. They eat it anyway. It is the closest available.

**Interior wine, unwatered** — served at the temperature of the room it was stored in, which in the cooler seasons means it arrives cold and in the warmer seasons means it arrives at something resembling the correct temperature. It is rough. It is improving, slowly, as the Interior vineyards establish themselves. The fact that it is not watered down is the point.

### THE INNER WARD AND THE SEAT

I ate in the Inner Ward twice, on occasions that were not entirely of my choosing, and I have secondhand accounts of what is served at the Seat itself from a source I will not name.

The Inner Ward formal meal is not so much about eating as about demonstrating the distance between here and the Outer Ring. Every ingredient signals this. The spice used on the fish comes from beyond Ath entirely — the trade cost of it is the point. The bread's whiteness is the point. The game bird, the cultivated greens grown in deliberate garden plots rather than foraged from the forest edge, the aged wine kept in proper conditions rather than drunk within a season of production — all of these things say: we have enough surplus to be particular.

There is a preparation served at Inner Ward formal occasions that is described to guests as a historical reconstruction. It is based on a Pre-Fall recipe text and uses the best approximation of ingredients that current cultivation can produce. It is served with some ceremony. It tastes, I am told by someone who has eaten both the reconstruction and something from a Pre-Fall source I will not elaborate on, almost correct. The *almost* is doing considerable work.

What is served at the Seat itself I know only from the account of one person who had occasion to dine there and found the experience, in their words, *less impressive than expected and more unsettling than it should have been*. They would not elaborate. The Emperor, I am given to understand, eats sparingly and with evident disinterest in the quality of what is placed before him. This may reflect ascetic discipline. It may reflect something else.

## THE NORTHERN VILLAGE

The northern Interior village inn — and most substantial northern villages have one, a building that functions as inn, drinking establishment, and community meeting space simultaneously — offers a table that is honest, seasonal, and shaped entirely by what the forest and the river and the winter stores provide.

**Rye pottage with foraged mushrooms** is, in my opinion, one of the genuinely undervalued preparations in Ath. The northern forests are rich with fungal variety and the village cooks know them with an intimacy that comes from generations of necessity. The rye grain base is the same as Antitheis, but the mushrooms — added in the morning, cooked slowly through the day — produce a depth of flavour that the city version entirely lacks. Several varieties I encountered in northern village cooking I did not see anywhere else on the continent.

**River fish, simply roasted** — the Interior rivers carry freshwater fish that are smaller and less dramatically flavoured than the Gulf mackerel but have their own clean, mild character that suits the northern preparations well. Roasted whole over fire, dressed with nothing more than salt and whatever herbs the cook had drying by the hearth. Eaten with rye bread.

**Dark rye bread** — the northern village bread deserves its own note. The starter cultures in some northern families are old enough that nobody remembers their origin. The slow fermentation produces a particular sour complexity that I found more interesting than the urban versions, which are produced on tighter schedules. The northern bread takes longer to make, keeps longer after making, and develops more flavour over its keeping period. I carried several loaves when leaving northern villages and found them excellent for the first four days of travel.

**Foraged berry preserve** is made in the late summer from the forest edge fruit — small, tart, reddish things that are probably the reverted descendants of cultivated varieties. The cooks preserve them in honey or in their own reduced juice. Eaten sparingly through winter on bread or stirred into pottage, they are one of the few sources of sweetness in the northern diet and treated accordingly.

**Village ale** is highly variable. Each northern village brews from available grain with local water and the accumulated wisdom or accumulated idiosyncrasy of whoever manages the brew. I had village ales in the north that were genuinely excellent, complex and well-balanced in a way that the standardised urban product is not. I had village ales that I will not describe further out of respect for the hospitality under which they were offered.

**Salted game** — rabbit, occasionally deer, small boar from the deeper forest — is the northern protein alongside river fish. The northern forests are good for game in a way the more trafficked Interior is not, and the preservation traditions are accordingly developed. Hung, salted, occasionally smoked over green wood from the forest. The smoked preparations in particular have a quality I found myself thinking about long after leaving.

**A bread made in a particular shape** is baked in northern villages at the turn of winter, and I mention it here because I encountered it in three separate villages across my time in the north and found it in all three cases to be identical in form. The bread is made in the shape of a sleeping figure — arms folded, eyes closed — from a slightly enriched dough, slightly sweeter than the daily rye bread. Nobody I asked could tell me the origin of the practice. It has always been done this way. The figure is made at the turn of winter, when the days are shortest, and eaten the following morning. Some families name the figure before baking it. They would not tell me what names they used.

I did not ask.
