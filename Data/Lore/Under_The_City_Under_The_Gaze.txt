# UNDER THE CITY, UNDER THE GAZE
### *An Account of the Undermarket of Antitheis*
*Krad Kraps*

---

*A note before I begin. I was allowed down into the Undermarket on the condition that I name no one living and mark no way in, and I have kept to it. What follows is therefore an account with no map and no cast, which is a poor sort of anthropology and the best I am willing to write. The people who showed me the place did so at real risk to themselves. I will not repay that with a description a clerk of the watch could use.*

*The name I have put on this chapter is not mine. It is a thing one of them said to me, late, when I asked how a person learns to live down there. You live under the city, he said, and under the gaze, and in time you get used to both. I have thought about it a great deal since. There is more than one gaze on the people below Antitheis, and I will come to each of them.*

---

## THE DEEP

Under Antitheis, as under much of Ath, the Pre-Fall world is still there. The city the Empire records in its ledgers is only the top of a thing that goes down further than the ledgers admit. Beneath the newer cellars and the sewer courses lie older workings: passages of dressed stone fitted too well to be anyone's afterthought, halls whose purpose no living person can name, and below those, the parts even the people who live down there will not go into without good reason. The salvage that comes up from the deep is the market's oldest trade. Cut stone, worked metal that has not rusted in all the time it has lain there, objects whose use is a matter of guesswork and sometimes of quiet argument. A great deal of what is sold in the Undermarket was made by hands that have been dust since before the erasure.

I will set down what the delvers told me and vouch for none of it. They speak of a cold in the lowest passages that is not the cold of stone and water, and of a sense, past a certain depth, of being counted. Of being *attended to* by something old and patient that means them no harm and no kindness either. The men who go deepest speak of this the way other men speak of weather, which unsettled me more than fear would have. I record it because an honest account of Ath cannot keep drawing a clean line between what a place is and what the people in it are certain it is. This continent does not respect that line, and I have stopped pretending it does.

## THE OLD UNDERMARKET

The Undermarket was not always a market. Those old enough to remember, and willing to speak of it, describe something else entirely.

When the Outer Ring was young, when the Empire's peace had made the roads safe enough that the scattered survivors of the hard years began coming back together and pouring into the capital faster than the capital could house them, a great many people arrived in Antitheis with nowhere to be. The Ring took most of them and threw up its warrens to hold them, badly and in a hurry, which is why it is the way it is to this day. But some went down. The dry upper passages under the city became a place to live for those the Ring itself had no room for, and over a generation or two it became something with a shape. Older people made it. It was, by every account I trust, a poor place and a decent one, closer to a village than a den, where people who had nothing arranged among themselves how to have a little of it together, and looked after each other's children, and kept the old year-turnings the Ring had stopped keeping. It was a refuge. I want to be plain about that word, because of what comes next.

## THE FIRE

Roughly twenty-five years ago, House Bituris burned it.

I will keep to what can be said. In the years House Bituris rose to its present height, it made a number of examples, and the Undermarket was one of them. The stated reason was that the place harboured subversives, unlicensed faith, people and practices the new order judged dangerous. The refuge was, by the standards of that order, guilty of all of it, in the way that any place the poor are left to run for themselves will always be guilty of harbouring whatever the powerful have decided to hunt that decade. The fire was not an isolated act. It belonged to the same few years, and the same appetite, as other examples I am not going to catalogue here. Those who could get out got out. A good many did not. I have spoken with people who were children in the old Undermarket and are not children now, and I have learned to change the subject before they do, because there is a particular flatness that comes into a voice at the edge of that memory, and I do not think it is mine to draw out for a reader's benefit.

## THE MARKET AS IT IS

What stands now was built in the ashes of that, by the ones who came up out of them.

The Undermarket today is a market in earnest, and a peaceable one, which given its history and its trade is a stranger achievement than it sounds. It is kept. There is a keeper, a woman who was a child of the old refuge and who took charge of what was left of it when she was barely grown, in years that were by all accounts a good deal bloodier than the present. Whatever it cost to make the place safe enough to trade in, it was paid long ago, and the woman who paid it has spent the decades since keeping it from ever needing to be paid again. She is not, whatever the Ring believes, a crime-lord out of a ballad. She is closer to what a market anywhere needs and rarely names aloud: the person who guarantees that a deal struck is a deal kept, so that thieves and desperate people and the merely cautious can do business without cutting each other's throats. She takes her cut. She permits a great deal. Two things she does not permit are blood on the market floor and the attention of the watch, and her memory for anyone who threatens either is very long.

The trade is everything the city above will not sell you. Salvage from the deep. Goods that fell off an honest cart. Papers for people who cannot come by them honestly. Medicines the Collegium keeps behind its own locked doors, and worse things than medicines. I bought nothing and judged nothing and was not asked to.

## THE WATCHMEN

Of everything I saw below, the thing that has stayed with me most is the least sinister, which is often the way.

The keeper knows what happens in the lower Ring and at the mouths of her market before it reaches her, and she knows it through the people the city has trained itself not to see. The homeless of the Ring, the ones a passing clerk's eye slides off without registering, are her eyes. Some are paid in coin and some in a meal and a blanket through the worst of winter, and a few have grown genuinely expert at it, able to tell her which unfamiliar face at which alley-mouth means trouble and which means only a lost soul looking for something to buy. They call themselves, with the dry humour of people who have decided to find their situation funny before anyone else can, the Watchmen. I found it one of the more remarkable folk institutions I have come across anywhere on Ath: an entire order of intelligence built out of exactly those people the Empire is most confident are of no account. The gaze the Empire keeps on the Undermarket is answered, quietly, by a gaze of its own.

## THE SAND

I would be dishonest by omission if I left out the pits, though I confess I would like to.

Blood-sport is the Undermarket's great entertainment and its great engine of coin. The trade has a shape worth setting down, because it surprised me with its orderliness. There are those who own the pits themselves and take the wagering and the sale of food and drink, and there are others, the organisers, who stage the shows and take the larger share of a given night's custom, and the two are not always the same people. The best slots, the crowded hours, go to the biggest draws, and a newcomer takes what is left. The marquee is the old-fashioned fighting, one trained man against another, and its finest practitioners are treated as something between champions and gods down there where the sun does not reach. A fighter brought in from foreign parts is a particular event, and the crowd gives such men names to chant, since it seldom troubles to learn their real ones. There is a fighter they call the Snowbear, out of the cold north, whose bouts I am told empty every other pit in the market. I did not see him fight. I was offered the chance and declined, and I will let the reader draw what conclusions they like about my anthropology.

For the pits are also where the market's decency runs out. Not every man on the sand is there by choice, and the fodder for the smaller, uglier shows includes people taken from other regions and sold into it, which is to say the trade in human beings is real down there and I will not soften it. The keeper is said to hold a line against the worst of it, and I believe she does, and I also believe that a line held is a thing that exists precisely because there is pressure against it. There is at least one organiser, small and hungry and by every description not well in his mind, who wants to stage crueller things than the keeper will allow, and goes about looking for a hidden room to stage them in. I mention it not as gossip but as the truest picture I can give of the place: a peace kept by one will, and the men already testing where that will ends.

## THE ACCOMMODATION

The Undermarket survives above ground the way it survives below it, by an arrangement with the gaze.

It is an open enough secret that elements of the watch are paid to look the other way, and that this accommodation is the single thing most responsible for the market's continued existence. It is also the market's deepest vulnerability, and everyone down there knows exactly which of its trades would end the arrangement overnight if it were ever forced into the daylight. This is why the keeper polices her own the way she does. She is not a moralist. She is a woman who watched this same place burn once for being careless with exactly this, and who does not intend to see it burn twice.

## THE YOUNG AND THE OLD

I will end on the fracture I saw running through the place, because it is the most human thing about it and the thing most likely to decide its future.

The people who remember the fire are cautious to their bones. They know precisely how large the thing above them is, and precisely what pushing against it costs, because they paid the invoice once and are still paying it. The young do not remember. They have known only the market's long dominance, and they mistake the caution of their elders for cowardice, and they talk, in the way of the young everywhere, about pushing back at the watch and the House and the whole weight of the Empire. Their elders shout them down, or try to, and cannot make them feel a fire they never stood in. I have seen this exact argument, in other forms, in a dozen places on Ath. It is never really an argument about tactics. It is an argument between people who have been burned and people who have not yet been, and it is only ever settled the one terrible way.

---

*I came up out of the Undermarket the way I imagine most people do, blinking, and more tired than the walk accounted for. I have tried here to record it honestly, which for a place like this means recording the decency and the cruelty in the same breath and refusing to average them into something more comfortable. The people below Antitheis built a life in the grave the Empire dug for them. That it is a hard life, and in places a monstrous one, does not make it less of an achievement, and that it is an achievement does not excuse a single thing done to put them there. Both are true. On this continent, both are almost always true, and I have stopped waiting for it to be otherwise.*

*— K.K.*
