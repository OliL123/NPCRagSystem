# STORIES IN CONSTRUCTION, VOLUME II
### *An Account of Antitheis, Imperial Capital*
*Krad Kraps*

---

I had not intended to write a second volume.

The first account was a survey — the continent of Ath considered broadly, its regions and their buildings, the architectural vocabulary of the Empire and its variations across the distance from the northern timber frontier to the Gulf Coast's volcanic stone. A scholar's overview. Something useful.

Then I spent three years in Antitheis.

What follows is not a survey. It is the record of someone who looked at one city for long enough that the city stopped being a subject and became a context — the thing inside which everything else was happening. The buildings of Antitheis are not interesting primarily as buildings. They are interesting as the physical record of an institution that has been making specific arguments about humanity and power and the nature of the world for two centuries, and that has built those arguments into stone that will outlast anyone currently alive to dispute them.

I have tried to describe what I saw and what I understood. Where I have not understood something I have generally said so. There are sections of this account where I have written around something rather than about it, which the attentive reader will notice and which I ask them to attribute to caution rather than ignorance. There are things in Antitheis that reward caution.

The companion volume covers the continent. This one covers the city. Between them they cover what I know. What I do not know is considerably larger, and the reader who finds the gaps in this account is welcome to fill them, if they can.

*— K.K., Antitheis, Year 43 of the Silver Era*

## THE UPPER CITY

### THE TRANSITION

There is no wall separating the upper city from the Mid City below, but there is a threshold you cross nonetheless. The streets become wider — not gradually, but at specific points where the terrain flattens and the buildings step back from the road with a deliberateness that feels architectural even when it isn't quite planned. The crowds thin. The noise of the Mid City market arcades falls away behind you and what replaces it is not silence exactly but a different register of sound — the wind from the high ground, the distant bells of the administrative towers, the particular quality of footsteps in wide empty streets.

Some of the Pre-Fall walls are visible here in a way they are not quite anywhere else in the city. In the Outer Ring and Mid City, ruins tend to be absorbed — built over, incorporated, reduced to their most useful elements. In the upper city, certain sections of Pre-Fall wall have been left standing to their original height, or close to it, for reasons that are officially aesthetic and practically political. They say: something was here before us and it was significant. We are the inheritors. The walls themselves are a different stone from the Viriman construction around them — darker, more precisely fitted, the surface carrying in places the ghost of carved detail that the Excision campaigns didn't quite finish removing. A hand, almost legible. An arch of something above a lintel, ground to smoothness that is itself conspicuous.

The upper city was not built to house people in the way the rest of Antitheis was. It was built to express an idea about power and about what power looked like, which is a subtly different function and produces a subtly different kind of space. The buildings are grand. The streets are wider than they need to be for the population that uses them. The public spaces are larger than they need to be for the events that occur in them. There is a permanent slight emptiness to the upper city that visitors notice and residents have long since stopped perceiving. It feels, at certain hours, like a city designed for a larger and more ceremonial civilisation than the one currently occupying it — which is not accidental. The Emperor has been building toward something for two centuries. The upper city is what that looks like from the outside.

This is the Renovatio in its architectural form: not the recovery of what was actually there before, but the construction of what should have been there, imagined through the lens of what the Pre-Fall world left behind and what the Imperial tradition chose to cite. The column shafts in the administrative buildings are sometimes Pre-Fall material, taken from the ruins and re-erected at the Emperor's direction. The proportions of the civic spaces reference what can be reconstructed from the old diagrams. The whole upper city is a deliberate argument about continuity with something that no longer fully exists, made by someone who remembers it more clearly than he lets on.

### THE STREETS AND PUBLIC SPACES

Upper city streets are paved in flagstone rather than cobble — flatter, smoother, easier to process officially. The main arterial roads are wide enough for four carts abreast, which is a statement about intention rather than traffic volume. Their edges are lined with the stone benches that have largely disappeared from the Outer Ring, still maintained here and occupied mainly by the administrative clerks who eat their midday meals in the open air when the weather permits.

The public squares of the upper city are the grandest open spaces in Antitheis. The largest, before the Rigosena, is paved in two colours of stone — the dark local limestone and a lighter grey brought from quarries several days' journey away — laid in a radiating pattern that draws the eye toward the Hall's entrance. This paving is a deliberate echo of Pre-Fall ceremonial spaces. It is also, for anyone paying attention, a very expensive and time-consuming piece of civic decorating that nobody specifically asked for and that the Emperor apparently considered important enough to commission anyway.

The administrative buildings of the Inner Ward — the Hall of Counting, the garrison headquarters, the offices of the various Imperial functions — are Romanesque in structure and deliberately unadorned in finish. The contrast with the Rigosena is intentional. The administrative machinery of the Empire is functional stone. The Emperor's court is something else.

### THE HALL OF RECORD AND ANTIQUITIES

The building that houses the Empire's civic library and public museum occupies a site adjacent to the Collegium and is, architecturally, the most unusual structure in the upper city. Everything else in Antitheis follows a legible spatial logic — the rotunda opens centripetally, the Mortalis Cathedral directs you axially toward the apse, the townhouse organises around the courtyard. The Memories Caged does none of these things. You enter it and immediately begin to lose your spatial bearings in a way that takes most visitors several visits to stop noticing.

The building's plan is octagonal — an inner octagon of piers supporting the upper structure, surrounded by an outer shell with a continuous ambulatory between them, two-storeyed galleries running along the inner face of the outer shell, and between each pair of inner piers a deep semicircular niche that is both structural and, by happy accident of proportion, exactly the right size for a reading alcove with a lamp. The result is a space that cannot be understood from any single position within it. You see part of it. You move. You see a different part of it that doesn't quite connect to the part you just left. The upper gallery level casts the lower floor into a variable shadow that changes through the day as the light through the high windows tracks across it. The structural logic, which is sophisticated beyond anything the Rebuilding period could have produced unassisted, is invisible from inside. The building reads as having grown rather than having been designed.

Its origin is Pre-Fall. The stonework of the inner octagon is a different grade from anything added subsequently — finer, more precisely fitted, the mortar joints barely visible. The outer shell and the galleries are Rebuilding-era additions, competent work that respects the original structure without quite matching it. The building's official history describes it as an expansion of a surviving Pre-Fall civic structure whose original function is not recorded. What it was originally is one of the questions in the Department of Immaterial Science's closed files.

**The library floors** occupy the ground level and the two gallery levels above it. The ground floor is the most public — open to any citizen with upper city access, which in practice means students, administrators, scholars, and the occasional Collegium visitor who has made the journey up. The collection here is extensive: civic records from the full Rebuilding period, administrative documentation going back to the first years of the Empire, maps and surveys, the published works of the Collegium's faculty, and a secondary collection of Pre-Fall documents in varying states of translation and legibility.

The first gallery level requires a scholarly credential — a Collegium affiliation, a house appointment, or a specific research permission issued by House Briganar. The collection here includes documents whose content is considered sensitive rather than classified — things that are not secret but that the Empire would prefer not to be widely read without context. The Pre-Fall geographical records are here. The partial annals of the societies that preceded the Empire. The fragmentary accounts of what the sky looked like for seventeen days during the Fall.

The second gallery level is closed. House Briganar staff work up there. Occasionally something comes down from it. Occasionally something goes up and does not come back down. The door at the top of the second staircase is iron-banded and undecorated and has been locked for as long as anyone currently working in the building can remember.

The deep semicircular niches between the inner piers are the building's most beloved feature among its regular users. Each is large enough for a small table and two people, or one person and considerable material spread across both chairs. The lamp bracket in each niche is original construction — Pre-Fall ironwork, the fitting for a fuel type that no longer exists, long since adapted to hold a standard oil lamp. The niches have a quality of enclosure and quiet that is difficult to achieve elsewhere in the upper city — the ambulatory behind them absorbs sound, the gallery above creates a psychological ceiling lower than the building's actual ceiling, the curves of the apse embrace rather than contain. Students from the Collegium treat specific niches as unofficially reserved through seniority and custom. A third-year student sitting in a niche occupied by a senior research fellow will be informed of their error, politely but immediately.

There are texts in this building that have not been read in decades, possibly longer. The cataloguing system — House Briganar's, meticulous in theory — has gaps that accumulate at the edges of classifications that nobody currently alive is certain how to apply. Documents that arrived in lots from the early Rebuilding period and were shelved pending review and never reviewed. Pre-Fall material that was translated enough to establish it was not obviously dangerous and then returned to its shelf. The Dreamers who use the library have learned, over years, where the catalogue's edges are. What they find there is not always what they expected.

### THE TRICONCH GALLERY

Attached to the Memories Caged by a covered passage on its northern face is a structure that would not exist in any Viriman architectural tradition — three semicircular apses of equal size arranged in a trefoil plan around a small central space, each opening into the next through wide arched doorways, the whole thing covered by three shallow domes of slightly different heights that create a roofline visible from the street that doesn't quite match any intuition about what the interior must look like.

The spatial experience is unlike anything else in Antitheis. You enter the central space and you are immediately pulled in three directions simultaneously. Each apse offers a different view, a different quality of light from its windows, a different collection of objects. There is no axis. There is no correct direction of movement. You orbit the central space and each return finds it slightly different depending on which apse you have just emerged from. Visitors who have been to the Memories Caged and found its octagonal plan disorienting report that the Triforna feels like its logical extreme — a space designed not to have a front.

The official function is a museum of material culture. In practice this means the objects that the Empire has decided are safe to display publicly — Pre-Fall ceramics and metalwork whose original devotional content has been either ground away or was abstract to begin with, Rebuilding-period craft objects of historical interest, things brought back from other continents through trade that demonstrate the Empire's commercial reach, commissioned Viriman-era work in the geometric abstract tradition.

The three apses are organised loosely by period — Pre-Fall in the eastern apse, Rebuilding in the western, and the Empire's mature period in the northern. The eastern apse is the most interesting and the most carefully managed. The objects there are the oldest and some of them are clearly fragments of larger things — a carved stone hand of considerable quality, its wrist ending in a clean break that suggests deliberate separation from the rest of whatever it came from. A panel of ceramic tile in a geometric pattern whose proportions and glazing technique the Collegium's material scientists cannot account for using any known process. Several objects whose surface carries text in the oldest dialect, transliterated but not yet translated, the transliteration itself held in the restricted first gallery.

The Triforna's Pre-Fall origin is not officially stated. The quality of the stonework makes it obvious to anyone with architectural knowledge. The Empire incorporated it into the civic complex because it was there, structurally significant, and its disorienting spatial qualities seemed, to whoever made the decision, more useful than threatening.

### THE CAMPANILES

Three bell towers stand in the upper city, each on slightly elevated ground, each separate from the buildings adjacent to them in the tradition of the civic campanile rather than the attached tower. Their bells ring at dusk — not simultaneously but in sequence, the easternmost first and the westernmost last, so that the sound rolls through the upper city over a span of perhaps twenty seconds, a wave of bronze that passes through rather than hitting all at once. From inside the wave, standing still in a street as it passes, you hear the sequence and you hear your own position within it.

The two older campaniles are Pre-Fall construction. Their stonework has the quality of the inner octagon of the Memories Caged — precisely fitted, the joints barely visible, the stone face showing a weathering pattern consistent with great age. The bells inside them are not original — the original bells were lost or taken during the Roving period and replaced during the Rebuilding. The replacement casting for the oldest tower carries an inscription around its rim in the oldest dialect. The text has been transcribed but not translated. House Briganar's position is that it is a dedication to the tower's commissioner, conventional for bell inscriptions of the period. The Dreamers' position is more interesting and less certain.

The third campanile is Rebuilding-era — solid, competent, the stonework slightly coarser, the proportions slightly less elegant than the Pre-Fall originals. It rings a fraction of a second later than the sequence requires and has always rung a fraction of a second late, which the tower's maintenance record attributes to a minor mechanical fault in the bell mechanism that has been noted in every inspection and never fully resolved.

The towers are accessible by a narrow spiral stair inside each one — tight enough that two people descending cannot pass two people ascending without negotiation. At the top, a small lantern room with open arched windows on all four faces and a stone bench running around the interior perimeter. The view from the oldest tower encompasses nearly the full extent of Antitheis — the Outer Ring's black roofscape to the horizon, the Mid City's ruin gardens and arcade streets in the middle distance, the ravine cutting through the city below with its bridges and the thread of water at its base, the Rigosena's floating dome visible from almost any vantage.

Collegium students use the campanile tops for the same reason people have always used high places that are technically public and practically private — the stair deters casual visitors and the wind at the top makes ordinary conversation difficult, which creates a quality of solitude that the rest of the upper city does not offer. The view produces the specific quality of thought that comes from seeing the thing you live inside from outside it. The city from above is not the city from within, and the difference is informative.

Students leave things in the lantern rooms. Not officially and not permanently, but a tradition has accumulated of leaving a small written thing — a poem, a fragment of argument, an observation — tucked into the gap between the stone bench and the wall. These accumulate until someone who is not a student notices them and removes them, which happens every few years. Then they accumulate again. The oldest tower's collection, before the last removal, included something written in a hand that the Collegium's palaeography faculty dated, when shown a copy, to early Rebuilding period. What it said has not been publicly shared.

The Dreamers use the campanile tops as informal gathering points — the narrow stair means that arrival and departure are visible, which is both a security risk and a social filter. You can see who is coming up before they arrive. The wind makes surveillance from outside the lantern room essentially impossible. The view means you notice anyone watching the base of the tower from the streets below. It is not a secure meeting place in any formal sense. It is better than most of the alternatives.

---

The upper city belongs to its workers before it belongs to anyone else.

In the last hour before dawn, when the Outer Ring is already loud with the earliest markets and the Mid City is still mostly dark, the gates and back passages of the upper city fill with a specific kind of traffic. Workers moving upward — kitchen staff, chamber attendants, gardeners, laundresses, the maintenance workers who keep the formal surfaces of the upper city in the condition that upper city residents expect to find them. They move quickly and quietly and in patterns so established that the morning patrol constables know most of them by face. The streets that will be composed and selective in three hours are, for this window, more densely trafficked than at almost any other time. The upper city's working day begins before the upper city wakes up.

This migration originates primarily in the Mid City. The workers who serve upper city households are mostly Mid City residents — they have the documentation, the presentation, the knowledge of upper city etiquette that permits them to operate in that environment without friction. They carry the right credentials. They know which entrance to use. They know how to be present without being visible in the way the households require.

The Outer Ring workers who make the journey are fewer and more specifically employed. They do the jobs the Mid City workers don't do — the chimney sweeps, the washroom cleaners, the workers who maintain the drainage systems and remove what accumulates in the least considered parts of grand buildings. The dirtiest and most invisible work in the upper city is done by the people most out of place in it. They arrive in the deepest part of the pre-dawn window, when even the constables are sparse, and they are gone before the morning light reaches the wide streets.

### THE UPPER CITY JOB AS ASPIRATION

For the youth of the Outer Ring, a position in an upper city household is one of the most coveted economic outcomes available. The pay is better than almost any other work accessible without formal credentials. The air is genuinely cleaner — this is not sentiment but physiology. Workers who spend their days in the upper city's open streets and private gardens and well-maintained interiors instead of the smoke-thickened passages of the Outer Ring are visibly healthier over years. Longer-lived. Less prone to the respiratory conditions that take Outer Ring workers in middle age. The physical difference between someone who has spent a decade cleaning upper city chimneys and someone who has spent a decade working a Outer Ring forge is legible in their faces.

The chimney sweeps' trade is the most specifically valued. Upper city chimneys are complex — the grand buildings have flue systems that are partly Pre-Fall infrastructure, partially modified over two centuries, connecting in ways that the buildings' current owners don't fully understand and that only the sweeps map comprehensively. The trade passes from parent to child or from master to apprentice with the specific knowledge — which chimneys share a stack, which flues connect properties that don't officially connect, which passages allow a thin person to move between parts of a building without using any door. This knowledge is professionally essential and incidentally extraordinary. The sweeps are the most intimate cartographers of the upper city's hidden structure.

The craft specialists who receive passes for specific upper city work — the glaziers who maintain greenhouse panels, the ironworkers who repair the decorative gates, the instrument makers called in to work on a private commission — carry the experience back to the Outer Ring as a credential. They were there. They worked for a name that even the Outer Ring has heard. This opens doors in their own communities that a Mid City commission does not.

### THE BACK PASSAGE ECONOMY

The servants' routes through the upper city — the delivery passages, the kitchen yard connections, the narrow back streets between compound walls — constitute a secondary geography that no official map acknowledges and that every worker knows intimately. The formal front of the upper city is designed to be seen. The back is designed to function, and function has produced a network of connections and daily routines that intersects every significant household in the district.

Information moves through this network as naturally as deliveries do. The kitchen worker who knows that a particular house's breakfast was served late this morning because a courier arrived at midnight. The laundress who knows what correspondence was spilled on and therefore what correspondence exists. The gardener who knows which greenhouse specimens were recently removed and which were recently acquired, which tells something about a house's recent diplomatic relationships with the Gulf trade routes. None of these people are spies in any formal sense. All of them talk to each other, and to their families in the Mid City, and to the bath house keeper who serves their street, and to the occasional person who buys them a drink and asks interested questions.

The interested questioners are a specific professional community. Information merchants — not affiliated with any house formally, compensated by multiple clients simultaneously for the same intelligence, careful about which of their clients they tell what. They operate in the Mid City bath houses and eating houses, positioned at the junction between the upper city's servant networks and the houses' appetite for knowing what their competitors are doing. The major houses all maintain counter-intelligence operations — Briganar through its census network, Bituris through its formal informant apparatus, Yambal through its commercial contacts — and all of them are also trying to identify and either recruit or neutralise the independent information merchants.

The chimney sweeps occupy a particular position in this ecosystem. Their access is too specific and too intimate to be replicated by any other channel. A house that wants to know the physical layout of a competitor's compound — which rooms connect to which, where the archive is, how the flue system was modified and when — finds that the most reliable source is the sweep who has worked both buildings. The sweeps know this. They are compensated accordingly. They are also very careful, as a professional community, about how far they extend this service and to whom, because the line between information merchant and asset of a powerful house is one that, once crossed, is difficult to walk back from.

### THE COUNTER-CULTURE

The upper city produces a specific kind of disaffected youth that does not exist in quite the same form anywhere else in Antitheis.

They have the Collegium education. They have access to the Department of Immaterial Science's published papers and the intellectual framework to understand what those papers are measuring without stating. They have grown up in a social world so precisely encoded that they can read the meaning of a coat's cut and the political implications of a dinner table placement before they can legally drink. And some of them, encountering all of this simultaneously, find that what they feel most strongly is the desire to go somewhere that none of it applies.

They go down. Where the Outer Ring children climb upward toward air and the freedom of being above the system, the upper city counter-culture moves deliberately in the opposite direction — into the Mid City ruin gardens, the bath house Catuvar dens, and eventually into the Outer Ring's Pits, where the Dockbeat is loud enough to make the carefully maintained social awareness of the upper city simply impossible. The noise is the point. You cannot perform your family's social position in a room where you cannot hear yourself.

What they find in the Pits is the Dockbeat tradition with its circumlocutory songs — and unlike the dock workers who have always sung them without fully theorising them, these visitors have the conceptual vocabulary to understand exactly what the songs are circling around. They come back to the upper city carrying something the Collegium's formal curriculum didn't give them. They start talking to each other about it.

### THE DREAMING

The movement has no official existence. House Bituris has a file. The file has no official title.

What exists is a loose network of upper city youth — primarily Collegium students and recent graduates, some minor house scions, a smaller number of workers' children who earned their way into Collegium admission and found the class proximity more radicalising than they expected — who share certain texts, attend certain informal gatherings, and use the word *dreaming* as both a descriptor and a greeting. The name is deniable. It is also unmistakable to anyone who understands what it invokes.

The movement is internally fractured in the way all movements of this kind are fractured, and the fractures are productive rather than terminal. Some members are primarily focused on the folk tradition question — that the Excision has destroyed the cultural commons, that the night soil collectors' practices and the sleeping-figure bread and the plateau burial rites are fragments of something coherent that should be understood and recovered rather than prosecuted and erased. Some are focused on the cosmological question — what the Department of Immaterial Science actually knows, what the belief-to-reality mechanic implies about the Empire's founding mythology, what it implies about the Emperor specifically. Some have read far enough into the historical record to have uncomfortable questions about House Carnaris. Some are primarily there for the cultural rebellion and find the theoretical discussions tedious, which the theorists find frustrating, which is probably healthy.

They pass texts hand to hand — some of them published Collegium papers with marginal annotations drawing the implications the authors were careful not to draw, some of them transcriptions of folk songs and documented practices gathered from the provinces, some of them things that technically shouldn't exist. The most dangerous texts in circulation are not ideological manifestos. They are careful, footnoted analyses of the Department of Immaterial Science's published data with the variables renamed to make the conclusions legible.

The movement's relationship with the lower city is complicated and class-inflected in ways its more self-aware members acknowledge without fully resolving. The Outer Ring workers whose traditions they are partly trying to recover are not their peers, and the dynamics of educated upper city youth arriving in the Pits to experience authentic Dockbeat culture are visible to the Dockbeat musicians in ways the visitors find uncomfortable when someone articulates them directly. The most honest members of the Dreamers understand that they are tourists in their own city's lower districts. Some of them find this understanding radicalising. Some of them find it paralysing.

House Bituris manages rather than suppresses the movement because suppression would drive it underground and make it harder to monitor. Several informants are embedded in its outer circles. The more paranoid members of the Dreamers suspect this. The less paranoid ones don't want to believe it. The internal debates about who can be trusted are as consuming as the debates about everything else, which is probably also not accidental.

The specific danger of the Dreamers — the thing that elevates it from monitored nuisance to genuine concern — is the combination of educational access and downward mobility. A disaffected minor noble who goes to the Pits and comes back is a social embarrassment. A disaffected minor noble who goes to the Pits, develops a theoretical framework for the cosmological stakes of the Excision, gains the trust of an Outer Ring community through years of consistent presence, and starts connecting the folk practitioners with the academic analysis — that is something the Empire has not fully dealt with before and is not certain how to.

The Emperor is aware of the Dreamers. He has been aware of movements like it before, in earlier iterations, under different names. He watches it with the patience of someone who has watched many things unfold over two centuries and understands that the question is never whether a thing like this exists but what it eventually becomes.

Every piece of Viriman architecture presses down. The rotundas spread outward. The thick walls contain rather than elevate. The windows are small. The eye is not encouraged upward.

The Rigosena does something no other building in the Empire does. The dome floats.

It achieves this through a ring of windows at the dome's base — not the small deeply-splayed openings of standard Viriman construction but a continuous band of glazing that floods the transition between wall and dome with light, making the structural join invisible. The dome appears to rest on nothing. It appears to have been placed rather than built. Standing at the Hall's centre and looking up — which the space specifically invites you to do, which is the one place in Antitheis where the eye goes upward without resistance — produces a sensation that people who have experienced it consistently struggle to describe. Not sacred, exactly. They know better than to use that word. Something else. Something that the Excision has not provided a vocabulary for.

Whether the Emperor designed this effect deliberately is a question the architectural record does not answer. The Hall was built in the early Rebuilding period, when documentation was sporadic. What the record does show is that the Emperor approved the final plans, and that those plans included the window ring. He has had two centuries to change it. He has not.

The exterior of the Hall is the largest building mass in the upper city — a wide drum supporting the dome, preceded by a columned portico whose columns are Pre-Fall material re-erected at the Emperor's direction. The originals stood in whatever complex occupied this site before the Fall. They have been ground smooth of their original decoration. The new inscriptions carved into them are administrative rather than devotional. They record decisions.

The interior is the city's most significant public space, used for the major ceremonies of the Imperial calendar — the formal investiture of house heads, the annual address to the administrative council, the reception of delegations from other continents. At these moments the Hall is full and the effect of the floating dome on a crowd is extraordinary. At other times the Hall is open to any citizen with the documentation to enter the Inner Ward, which in practice means a specific and relatively small part of the population. On an ordinary afternoon it is mostly empty and the dome's light falls on no one in particular, which has its own quality.

### THE GREAT HOUSE COMPOUNDS

The five houses cluster around the Rigosena in a rough semicircle, their compounds separated from each other and from the Imperial space by streets and gardens that maintain the fiction of independence while making the proximity to power readable to anyone who looks at a map.

**House Catubrix** occupies the compound directly northeast of the Hall, which gives them the optimal line of approach to the Inner Ward's main gate and is presumably not accidental. The building is the most fortified private structure in the upper city — not because Catubrix faces any immediate physical threat, but because fortress architecture is the only vocabulary they have. The gate is the architectural centrepiece. Everything else serves the gate. Visitors approach it and understand immediately that entry is at someone else's discretion.

**House Briganar** sits east of the Hall, and their compound is the most internally complex — multiple wings added over successive generations as the archive grew, windows that appear on every face of every building in quantities that make structural sense only if you understand that the occupants need light for reading at all hours. From the outside it looks like a building that has been extended thoughtfully and repeatedly by people who were always running out of space for one more thing they needed to keep.

**House Bituris** is northwest of the Hall, positioned such that its primary face looks inward toward the Imperial complex rather than outward toward the city. The windows on its outer faces are notably fewer than on Briganar. The building's mass is present and significant but its expression is turned away. Visitors to the compound approach it through a short passage between walls rather than across an open forecourt, which produces a specific psychological effect that is certainly intentional. You are inside before you have quite decided to enter.

**House Yambal's** Antitheis compound is the smallest of the five — their real seat is at Yamhon on the Gulf, and the Antitheis presence is maintained for political necessity rather than genuine occupation. What it lacks in scale it compensates for in finish. The stonework is the most precisely dressed in the upper city, the ironwork at the entrance is the most accomplished, and the small garden behind the main building is the only private garden in the upper city that receives enough attention to actually be beautiful. House Yambal spends money on the Antitheis compound the way wealthy travellers furnish their secondary residences — with the particular care of people demonstrating that the secondary residence does not reflect their full resources.

**House Ambarris** is southeast of the Hall, and their compound is the newest construction in the upper city by some margin. This is visible. The stone is lighter in colour than the weathered grey of everything surrounding it — it has not had time to darken — and the proportions are slightly larger than the compound's administrative footprint requires. The entrance arch is a little too wide. The forecourt is a little too spacious. The building is trying, in every dimension, to look as though it has always been there, and the effort is legible to anyone who has spent time in the upper city and learned what always-been-there actually looks like.

### ONCE LOST FOREVER FOUND — THE LOST

There is a path from the Rigosena's eastern entrance, covered and colonnaded, that leads to the building the city calls The Lost. The formal name above its entrance — Once Lost Forever Found — is carved in the oldest official script, the letters worn at their edges by two centuries of weather but still fully legible. Nobody uses the formal name in daily speech. Everyone knows what The Lost means without being told.

It is open to any person who can reach it. No documentation. No fee. No appointment. In a district that controls access to almost everything, The Lost is the one place that closes its door to nobody. The constabulary on morning patrol nod to the people they pass heading there at first light. The upper city residents who would not acknowledge an Outer Ring visitor on any other street hold the door for them here.

**The entrance** is a single wide doorway with no threshold step — the floor level is continuous from outside to inside, which matters immediately because the floor is carved with names. You do not cross a threshold into The Lost. You simply arrive and discover that the ground beneath your feet is no longer anonymous.

The path through the building is marked by specific stones — worn smooth by centuries of respectful feet, slightly raised from the surrounding floor surface, spaced for a walking pace that is slower than ordinary movement. You cannot cross The Lost at a stride. The building's geography requires you to step where it tells you, which means looking down at each step, which means seeing continuously the names you are moving among. The discipline is not enforced by any rule. It is enforced by the understanding that to step off the path is to step on someone.

Children are taught this before they are brought. The teaching is taken seriously.

**The interior** is not one space but many — a central hall with dividing walls extending inward from the sides, each wall creating a narrower chamber that contains you on three sides with names. The dividing walls do not reach the ceiling. The space above is open and continuous, shared across all the chambers simultaneously, which means you can hear — faintly, filtered through stone — the presence of other visitors in sections you cannot see. A murmur. The specific quality of silence that exists when people are being quiet together rather than simply being alone.

Every surface that can receive inscription has received it. The floor within the marked path. The walls to their full height on both faces. The columns. The ceiling of the lower ambulatory. The faces of the dividing walls. Two centuries of careful cutting, the earliest names in a larger script because there was space then, the later additions progressively smaller as the available surface diminished. In certain sections the names are so densely cut that they overlap — the edges of letters sharing stone with the edges of other letters, individual names requiring close attention to distinguish from their neighbours.

There are not enough names. Everyone who has thought about it understands this. What covers every surface of The Lost represents a fraction of what was lost — the dead whose existence could be confirmed from the fragmentary records of the Roving period, the survivors who remembered specific individuals and reported them to the early Rebuilding's recording project. The vast majority of those who died in the Fall have no name here. The names that exist are the edge of the absence.

**The seats** are stone, built into the walls at intervals throughout the chambers — low benches set into the stone at a height that allows long sitting, smoothed by use, occasionally worn with the specific hollows that accumulate where the same spot is chosen repeatedly by different bodies over many years. People come to The Lost to find a specific name and then sit with it for some time. People come who have not found the name they were looking for and sit anyway. People come who are not looking for anyone specific and simply sit, which the building accommodates without question.

The quality of the sitting is different from any other sitting in the upper city. In the dinner houses you sit to be seen. In the Collegium you sit to work. In The Lost you sit because you need to be somewhere that holds the weight of what happened without asking you to do anything about it. The building has been holding that weight for two centuries and will continue to hold it. This is its function and it performs it without ceremony.

**The basin** is at the building's centre, where the main hall widens before the apse. It is bone-dry — has always been bone-dry in anyone's recorded memory — and is made from a stone that is not local to Ath, pale and fine-grained, showing no weathering despite its evident age. It sits at floor level, its rim barely raised above the surrounding stone, its interior smooth and empty. The names of the floor continue up to its edge and stop. Whatever the basin was built for, it was not built to hold the names of the dead. It was built for something else entirely and the city grew its memorial around it.

The partial inscription on the basin's inner rim — "He dreamt everything you are. He will dream everything you will be. Do not wake him" — is in the oldest dialect, transliterated by the Collegium's faculty and provided on a small panel nearby in current script. The translation is straightforward. The custodians of The Lost describe the inscription as a Pre-Fall prayer to an unnamed creative divinity, preserved as a historical document. The Dreamers who visit — and they visit regularly, more regularly than their numbers would suggest — read the panel and say nothing and sit for a long time.

I have been to The Lost four times. On my third visit I sat for longer than I intended and was still there when the afternoon light changed and the names on the western wall caught a specific angle of illumination that made the cutting visible in a way it is not at other hours — the shadows in each letter suddenly deep, the names standing out from the stone as if they had been recently cut rather than centuries old. The effect lasted perhaps twenty minutes. Other visitors stopped what they were doing to watch it happen. Nobody spoke. When the light shifted and the names receded back into the stone, a woman near me exhaled — not a sigh exactly, more the sound of someone setting down something heavy — and then got up and left.

I recorded this in my notes that evening and found I had very little else to say about it.

### LIFE IN THE UPPER CITY STREETS

The upper city is not empty. It is selective. The people who move through its streets are fewer than anywhere else in Antitheis but they move with a specific confidence that comes from knowing they belong in a space that excludes most people by default. The crowding of the Outer Ring, the purposeful bustle of the Mid City market streets — these are replaced by something that feels almost theatrical in its composure. Wide streets, unhurried pace, clothing that announces social position in the precise vocabulary of upper city fashion. The occasional litter carrying someone too important to walk, or too aware of their importance to walk.

The streets smell better than below. The drainage is properly maintained. The cobbles are replaced by flagstone on the main thoroughfares and swept daily by workers whose sole function is to keep these specific streets in the condition the upper city expects. The oil lamps at the street corners are refilled on a reliable schedule. The public notice boards carry different information from the ones in the Mid City — not administrative announcements and market prices but the social calendar of the upper city, formatted for an audience that already knows the relevant prices.

In the evenings, particularly on days following the main social calendar events, the streets carry a different kind of traffic — groups moving between dinners and performances and private gatherings, the specific sound of people conducting themselves in public while being aware they are being observed. This is when the upper city feels most alive and most artificial simultaneously.

### THE DINNER HOUSES

The finest eating establishments in Antitheis are in the upper city, and their social function extends considerably beyond food. A table at the right establishment on the right evening is a form of political positioning. Who you sit near, who acknowledged you when you entered, whether you were seated before or after a particular house representative — these things are registered, discussed, and occasionally acted upon.

The cooking here is the most technically accomplished on Ath — not just the best ingredients, which money can obtain, but the accumulated expertise of kitchens that have been developing their tradition for two centuries. The reconstructed fruit preparations appear here in their most refined forms. Imported spice is used with the restraint that marks genuine skill rather than ostentatious spending. The black sauce of the Gulf Coast arrives in preparations that bear almost no resemblance to its dock-town origins. The plateau dried meats, which the Outer Ring eats as working rations, appear here in preparations that require a day of careful treatment and cost accordingly.

The rooms are intimate — never more than eight or ten tables, the lighting calibrated to be flattering and slightly dramatic, the service conducted with a precision that requires extensive training and produces a specific kind of invisible competence. You are not aware of being served. Things arrive and depart at the correct moment.

The proprietors of these establishments occupy a peculiar social position — not noble, not administrative, but possessed of information and access that both categories require. A dinner house keeper who has been operating for twenty years has heard things at adjacent tables that House Bituris would pay significantly for. Most of them have a comfortable understanding with House Bituris already. This is simply the cost of operating.

### THE COLLEGIUM

The Empire's only institution of formal higher learning occupies a compound in the upper city between the Inner Ward and the house district. It was established in the second century of the Rebuilding at the Emperor's direction — the official account describes this as a recognition that the Empire required trained administrators and would benefit from formalised expertise. This is accurate and incomplete.

The Collegium teaches mathematics, natural philosophy, engineering, biological science, history in the officially approved form, and the administrative disciplines that feed directly into House Briganar's clerical corps. These departments are conventional and well-regarded. Their graduates populate the middle ranks of the Imperial apparatus and the senior ranks of the great houses' support staff.

The **Department of Immaterial Science** is the Collegium's most closely watched faculty. It studies what the Empire officially describes as the latent energetic substrate of material reality — the forces that underlie the physical world and that appear, under certain conditions, to be influenced by collective human cognition and behaviour. The department does not use the word magic. It does not use the word belief. It publishes papers with titles like *On the Correlative Properties of Distributed Attentional Systems* and *Substrate Variance in High-Density Social Environments*. The papers are accurate, technically rigorous, and would be explosive in the hands of someone who understood what they were actually measuring.

House Bituris maintains three faculty positions in the Department. This is presented as institutional support for important research. The faculty in question publish less frequently than their colleagues and attend more administrative meetings.

The Collegium also houses a **Conservatory** — the only institution on Ath that formally trains composers and performers in the classical tradition. Admission is competitive and the training is years-long. The most accomplished graduates become the house musicians and ceremonial composers whose work fills the Rigosena and the great house reception rooms. A small number become the city's most significant cultural figures — known by name throughout Antitheis in a way that crossing class lines, their reputations carrying even into the Outer Ring, where the Dockbeat musicians who have never heard their formal compositions know their names anyway.

### THE FORUMS AND PUBLIC LIFE

The upper city has three major public open spaces beyond the paved precinct of the Rigosena. Each serves a distinct function and each has its own social character.

**The Stoa** is the oldest of the three — a colonnaded rectangular space with a covered portico along its southern face, beneath which speakers address whoever has gathered in the open area before them. This is the direct heir of the Roman and Greek philosophical public debate tradition, maintained here in a form the Collegium recognises as academically serious and the broader upper city population uses as entertainment. The most celebrated Collegium debaters are public figures in the upper city in the way that Catuvar champions are public figures in the Mid City — known, argued about, attended by regular audiences who have opinions about their technique.

The debates are moderated by senior Collegium faculty and technically subject to the same Excision constraints as all public speech — divine names may not be invoked. In practice the upper city debate tradition has developed a philosophical vocabulary sophisticated enough to discuss almost anything while maintaining strict formal compliance. The most accomplished debaters can construct an argument that touches the full cosmological stakes of the Empire's founding philosophy without naming a single deity, and the audience, who are educated enough to follow every implication, fills in what is never said. House Bituris monitors the Forum. They do so by attending, and looking interested, and saying nothing.

**The the Bowl** — named for the three terraced levels that step down from the upper city grade into a semi-sunken amphitheatre cut from the high ground — is the city's primary venue for formal dramatic performance. The design is deliberately earth-cutting rather than earth-piling, consistent with Viriman architectural logic: the space goes into the ground rather than up above it. The tiers of stone seating accommodate perhaps eight hundred people comfortably and a thousand uncomfortably. The acoustic properties are excellent.

The plays performed here are the highest expression of the circumlocutory dramatic tradition. The writers who produce work for this space are craftsmen of the gap — of the thing that is described precisely enough to be unmistakable and never named. A performance in the the Bowl of a well-regarded piece about a city that forgot something important will empty every dinner house in the upper city for its duration. The audience discusses it for weeks afterward in terms that never technically violate anything.

The Forum also hosts the upper city's formal duels. On days when a duel is scheduled the terraces fill as completely as they do for theatre, and the social protocols around witnessing and commenting on a duel are nearly as developed as those around reviewing a performance. The duel is announced in advance through the social calendar boards. Seconds are appointed by both parties. The outcome is formally recorded by a House Catubrix officer. The betting — conducted discreetly and through established intermediaries rather than openly — is substantial.

### CONSTABULARY AND ORDER

The upper city is policed by a dedicated civil constabulary that operates independently from House Catubrix's military garrison, though both answer ultimately to the Imperial structure. The constables are recognisable by their grey cloaks with black shoulder trim — not the full black-bordered grey of the Eastern March, but a reference to it that positions them as an urban enforcement arm with military adjacency.

Constable stations are located at regular intervals through the upper city — small stone buildings of two rooms, one for administration and one for holding, positioned at the junctions where the main arteries meet. They maintain the social order of the upper city in a way that is more formal and considerably more legible than the informal authority structures that govern the Outer Ring. An altercation in the upper city produces a constable within minutes. The constables know every significant resident of their patrol area by name and face. They are polite to those residents and something considerably less than polite to anyone who appears not to belong.

The question of who is permitted in the upper city is technically answered by the baptisterium registration system — upper city residency requires documentation that not everyone can obtain. In practice the constabulary exercises discretion, which means that money and the appearance of money produce a different experience of this discretion than their absence.

### FOUNTAINS

Water in the upper city is a demonstration as much as a utility. The major fountains in the public squares are fed from a dedicated aqueduct system — clean water from a source well outside the city's drainage influence, maintained by House Ambarris and representing one of the house's most visible demonstrations of its value to the upper city's quality of life.

The fountain bowls are carved from stone that is not local — brought at considerable expense from quarries whose material produces a specific pale grey that photographs the upper city's water against a background entirely different from the dark limestone of the surrounding buildings. The decoration is non-figural in accordance with Viriman convention — geometric banding, palm-and-fist motifs at the bowl rim — but the oldest fountains carry worn traces of something under the official decoration that nobody has found it worth the effort to completely remove.

The fountains are social gathering points in a way that differs from the Gospel wells of the Outer Ring. Nobody depends on them for clean water. They exist to be beautiful and to demonstrate that the upper city can afford beauty that serves no function, which is its own demonstration of function.

### THE CIVIC CEREMONIAL SPACES

Beyond the Mortalis Cathedral and the Rigosena, the upper city contains several smaller ceremonial structures that occupy the position temples would hold in a less carefully administered society. They are not temples. The Empire is precise about this. They are Halls of Memory, Chambers of Record, spaces of civic reflection.

They look like temples. The Empire is also precise about not discussing this.

The oldest of these, the The Open Palm, occupies a site that pre-Fall records suggest held something significant — the Collegium's historians believe it was a major religious complex of the old pantheon, though they publish this belief carefully and with extensive qualification. The current building incorporates what survived of the original structure in a way that is simultaneously a citation and an erasure: the original columns support the new roof, the original floor plan determines the new layout, and the original carved surfaces have been ground to a flatness that is now itself centuries old, worn smooth by the hands of people who run their fingers along blank stone without knowing why.

These spaces are used for the most significant Imperial ceremonies — the swearing-in of house heads, the formal declaration of Imperial policy changes, the moments when the apparatus of the Empire is called upon to be more than administrative machinery. The upper city maintains them with the seriousness of people who have decided, without quite admitting it, that some spaces require a different quality of attention.

### THE LUXURY TRADE DISTRICT

Along the two streets that run between the Collegium and the main forum, the upper city concentrates its specialist craft and trade. These are not market streets in the Mid City sense — no stalls, no Saturday opening, no Outer Ring traders arriving to hawk cheaper goods at the edges. Each establishment occupies a permanent premises with a discrete entrance and no need for a brightly painted sign to attract attention, because the clientele already knows where to go.

The instrument makers here produce the most technically refined Brass instruments in the city — not the improvised workshop pieces of the beer garden circuit but carefully calibrated objects that respond differently and cost accordingly. The jewellers work primarily in contrasting stone combinations — pale limestone set against dark basalt, or the Gulf's volcanic material set against Interior grey — producing pieces that are distinctly Viriman rather than imitating the gold-and-gemstone traditions of other continents. The cloth merchants carry silk in quantities and qualities that do not exist in the Mid City, and the deep indigo of Imperial dress is available here in gradations that encode rank with a precision that requires knowledge to read.

The tailors are the most socially significant craftsmen in the district. Upper city fashion is a language, and the tailors are its grammarians. A cut that signals Briganar administrative alignment without formally declaring it. A colour combination that places the wearer in a particular house's social sphere without binding them formally to it. The ability to read these signals and dress accordingly is one of the competencies the upper city assumes in anyone who appears in it, and the tailors who help people acquire that competency — who understand both the technical and social vocabularies they're working in — occupy a position of quiet but genuine influence.

### THE BOTANICAL GARDENS

Every significant upper city residence maintains a private garden, and the competition between these gardens is one of the upper city's more earnest social contests. The Yambal compound's garden is the most famous — the only one accessible to visitors with the right introduction, the only one that grows specimens brought directly from the Gulf trade routes and maintained by workers whose specific expertise is plants that do not naturally belong this far north. A plant that flowers in Yambal's garden before it appears in anyone else's is a minor diplomatic event noted in the social calendar.

The greenhouses attached to the larger compounds are their own architectural statement — timber and glass construction requiring craft knowledge that the city's glaziers maintain in a small and carefully managed professional community. At night, lit from within, they glow warm against the dark stone of the upper city with an effect that nobody designed for and that has become one of the characteristic images of the upper city after dark. The warmth of the greenhouse light visible from the street as you pass. The knowledge that inside something is growing that most of Antitheis has never seen and will never taste.

The rarest cultivated specimens are the reconstructed orchard varieties — the fruits that existed before the Fall in better forms than their wild descendants and that the wealthiest upper city households have been attempting to rebuild through patient cultivation and the guidance of Pre-Fall texts. No one has fully succeeded. Several have gotten close enough that the result is genuinely excellent while being not quite what the texts describe. The difference between what was and what has been reconstructed is one of the things you taste if you eat in the upper city's finest dinner houses and know enough to notice.

---

The third tier of Antitheis's musical culture belongs entirely to the upper city, and it is separated from Dockbeat and Nightbrass by more than geography. This is formal composition — music that requires years of training to perform, that is written down and preserved and studied, that exists in a tradition of technical development that the dock workers and the ruin garden groups have no access to and almost no awareness of.

The organ of the Mortalis Cathedral is the primary instrument of this tradition, in the ceremonial sense. But the chamber music that circulates through the great house compounds and the reception halls of the Inner Ward is performed on smaller ensembles — strings, the more refined brass instruments, voices trained specifically for the acoustic properties of stone rooms. The compositions are long, structurally complex, emotionally vast in a way that the compressed directness of Dockbeat is not and does not attempt to be. Where Dockbeat hits immediately and Nightbrass settles gradually, this music takes time to establish what it is doing and expects the listener to give it that time.

Access to this music is controlled by the same mechanisms that control access to everything else in the upper city. The houses host it. The Imperial court hosts it. The most accomplished performers in the city live and work here, at the pleasure of institutions that fund their practice and determine where they perform. The Outer Ring does not hear this music. The Mid City hears it occasionally, through the walls of the Mortalis Cathedral during major ceremonies, in the form of sound more than music — the bass registers of the cathedral organ carrying through stone into the adjacent streets in a way that makes the body aware of something significant without the mind quite grasping what.

*What follows concerns the residential fabric of Antitheis's Outer Ring specifically, as it represents the most extreme expression of domestic architecture on Ath. The principles here scale down — with considerable variation — to the interior towns and northern villages, treated in later sections.*

### THE SHAPE OF OVERCROWDING

The Outer Ring was never designed for its current population. It is a city built for perhaps two hundred thousand that now houses close to a million, and the architecture reflects this not as a planned response but as accumulated emergency. Buildings that were single-family have been subdivided vertically and horizontally. Floors have been added until the structural logic of the original is invisible beneath the accretion. Neighbouring buildings have merged at upper floors until the street below is essentially a tunnel. Some passages in the deepest Outer Ring have not seen direct sunlight in living memory.

The building material throughout is the dense grey limestone of the Viriman tradition, darkened by two centuries of continuous smoke from cooking fires, heating fires, the smiths and tanners and dyers who operate at street level, and the charcoal sellers. The stone has gone from its natural grey to a deep uniform black in the oldest sections. The only exceptions are recent repairs — pale new stone sits against the black of everything surrounding it with an uncanny visibility. You can read the history of every building by the colour of its patches.

Upper floors overhang the street on timber brackets, each generation extending slightly further than the last, until in the narrowest lanes the eaves of opposing buildings nearly touch overhead. Light reaches the street below for only a few hours around midday in summer. In winter it barely reaches at all.

### ROOFLINES AND THE PREMIUM OF SKY

The dominant roof form is the gable — a steeply pitched A-frame in fired clay tile, necessary for rain runoff. Cheaper buildings use wooden shingles, which are a significant fire risk. In the densest sections, opposing gables create valleys between buildings that accumulate water and debris. Over time these valleys get roofed over informally, the space beneath incorporated as additional habitable area. The original roofline becomes a floor. A second informal city exists above the street-level city, built from salvage, connected by improvised bridges and ladders, inhabited by people who have found that the only available space is above everything else.

Roof spaces are the most coveted informal territory in the Outer Ring. Not owned, not registered with House Briganar, simply occupied by whoever gets there first and can hold it. The smoke thins above the roofline. On clear days you can see the Rigosena's dome from the top of a well-positioned ridge.

The dominant youth activity on the roofscape is board sledding — running boards, salvaged planks, anything flat enough to ride — down the soot-slicked tile runs of the longer gable slopes. Specific runs have informal names. Particular descents are legendary. The crashes are occasionally fatal, which is partly the point. House Catubrix periodically attempts enforcement at street level. By the time anyone climbs to where the sledding is happening, the sleds are gone.

The rooftop kids know the topology of the Outer Ring from above better than any official cartographer — which roofs are structurally compromised, which sections connect at third-floor level, which buildings are accessible from which starting points. This knowledge has value beyond recreation.

### THE DARK BELOW

Mirroring the rooftop culture is the underground culture — the opposite response to the same claustrophobia. Where the rooftop kids go up toward air and visibility and performance, the underground community goes down into the Pre-Fall tunnel networks beneath the city, into sections that predate the current drainage infrastructure and were incorporated into it without being properly mapped.

These are the passages where Blaedre is most present, though nobody who uses them knows this. They know only that certain tunnels feel different — that the air in some sections is better than it should be, that specific passages have a quality difficult to name. The underground community treats the better passages as assets. They do not ask why they are better.

The two cultures — rooftop and underground — have mutual contempt and mutual fascination. People who move between both worlds are rare and extremely useful for things requiring speed and invisibility simultaneously.

### STREETS AND SANITATION

The Outer Ring streets are cobbled in roughly dressed limestone setts, laid with a slight camber that channels rainwater toward gutters at the street edges. In practice the infrastructure is overwhelmed and the streets flood in heavy rain, the water mixing with everything else that runs in the gutters.

Waste management operates through cesspits dug into shared courtyards, emptied by the night soil collectors — workers who operate after dark, carting waste out through designated city gates before dawn and selling it to Heartlands farms as fertiliser. The night soil collectors maintain folk practices around their work: specific words said before handling waste, cleaning rituals afterward, habits that evolved because workers who didn't follow them died at higher rates. Nobody calls these prayers. They are marginally effective in ways that purely practical precautions don't entirely explain. Night soil collectors are counterintuitively among the healthier Outer Ring workers despite operating in the most contaminated conditions.

### WATER AND THE GOSPEL WELLS

Clean water access is the central material problem of Outer Ring life. The Gospel of Man maintains a network of clean wells throughout the district — the most practically significant charity the institution performs. The water is real, clean, and the queues are kept orderly. Standing in queue for an hour also means an hour's exposure to the morning sermon. Nobody is required to listen. Almost everyone does, because there is nowhere else to look.

House Bituris informants operate near the wells not to watch for obvious heresy but to observe social dynamics — who talks to whom while waiting, who avoids the queue entirely, who seems uncomfortable during the sermon.

The people who avoid the wells drink from other sources. The underground water is not clean by any standard. In specific passages it is better than it should be by a margin nobody can account for.

### THE HERB GARDENS

Small cultivated spaces, fiercely protected, scattered wherever a structural collapse or cleared plot has left accessible soil. The apothecary who maintains one commands enormous informal social authority. The ill come to sit in them. The weak come to rest.

Plants grown here with genuine collective belief in their properties develop mild efficacy that plants grown without that investment do not quite match — though no one could demonstrate this formally. The garden is simultaneously providing medicine and, unknowingly, generating a small amount of the old cosmological exchange that the Excision was designed to eliminate. The apothecaries who maintain them tend to be old, to know the old names for things, to carry knowledge that was never written down and therefore never subject to Excision enforcement. They do not call what they know religion. They call it practice.

### THE CAGE HOMES

In the deepest airless passages, where light barely reaches and the smoke never fully clears, are the cage homes — subdivided rooms behind timber lattice or iron bar partitions, each space barely large enough for a sleeping mat and a hook for belongings. A room shared between eight or ten such partitions, the partitions not reaching the ceiling, no privacy, no security.

These are occupied by the transient, the recently arrived, people who lost their registered housing, people who fell through House Briganar's census entirely. House Briganar knows they exist in the aggregate — the census shows more people than registered housing can accommodate — but specific locations are difficult to enforce against.

People who live in cage homes have the highest rates of respiratory disease, the highest incidence of bone deformities from insufficient sunlight, and the shortest lifespans of any population in the city. The census data captures this if anyone looks at it carefully enough. Looking at it requires caring about the answer.

## DOMESTIC ARCHITECTURE — THE MID CITY

The Mid City is what the Outer Ring is not. The same material — dressed grey-to-black limestone, the Romanesque grammar of rounded arches and thick walls — but applied with intention rather than desperation. You can see the sky. The streets have a logic to them. Light reaches the pavement properly for most of the day, which after the Outer Ring feels almost excessive.

### THE TOWNHOUSE

The basic domestic unit is two to three storeys of dressed limestone, the Viriman architectural grammar applied deliberately. The ground floor is almost always commercial — a workshop facing the street, wide enough for the craftsman to work visible to passers-by, a thick wooden shutter that folds down to become a counter. Above the workshop entrance, carved into the stone lintel, the House Briganar registration mark — a small stamped seal confirming the property is officially recorded. Buildings without it are noticed. The upper floors are residential — the family that works below usually lives above.

The facade is relatively plain by other cultures' standards. What ornamentation exists concentrates at the entrance arch — the rounded arch above every doorway, sometimes with simple geometric carving, occasionally an ironwork gate of the kind Viriman smiths do well. Windows are deeply splayed — the wall thick enough that the window reveal is almost as deep as it is wide, giving the interior a particular quality of narrow-sourced light that spreads inward.

Behind every Mid City townhouse is a small courtyard. Not a garden in any prosperous sense — a functional space where work spills out, deliveries arrive, a private well if the family can afford it. In warmer months the courtyard is where meals are eaten and domestic life actually happens. The street facade presents the formal registered face. The courtyard is where things are real.

### THE STREETS AND ARCADES

The main commercial thoroughfares are four to six metres wide — enough for two carts to pass with argument. Still organic, still following terrain rather than any plan. The cobbles are better maintained than the Outer Ring, the drainage cleaner though not immaculate.

Some of the main commercial streets have arcades — colonnaded walkways built into the ground floors of facing buildings, the pavement covered by a continuous series of Romanesque arches so that you can walk the length of a market street without stepping into rain. These arcades are the commercial heart of the Mid City. The stalls and workshops fronting onto them are the most expensive trading positions in the district. The arcade creates a specific social quality — it slows foot traffic, it shelters conversation, it makes the street into something closer to a room.

Each significant Mid City district organises around a square — not a planned piazza but an organic widening where streets converge at a natural flat point in the terrain. Flagstone rather than cobble. The buildings facing the square are the most prosperous of the district. The inn faces the square almost invariably. The inn sign — wrought iron bracket, painted wooden board — is one of the few places in the Mid City where colour exists prominently against the grey stone and brown timber and black ironwork. A brightly painted inn sign stands out with genuine drama.

### THE PRE-FALL RUINS AND RUIN GARDENS

In the Mid City, Pre-Fall foundations break the surface in several places. Unlike in the Outer Ring where ruins get built over immediately, the Mid City has enough breathing room that some sites have been left partially exposed — not from historical sentiment but because the stone is too well-fitted to easily demolish and the site sits awkwardly for new construction.

Some have been developed into what residents call ruin gardens — open-air spaces enclosed by Pre-Fall walls, foundations cleared, tables and benches installed, a tap from a local brewery serving ale and rough wine. The closest thing the Mid City has to a beer garden, functioning as the primary social space for the surrounding district. The Pre-Fall arch serves as a frame someone has strung dried flowers through. The carved surfaces, where they haven't been ground smooth by the Excision campaigns, are worn from two centuries of hands running along them without knowing why. Vines grow over the upper sections. In warm months these spaces are full from late afternoon until the dusk bell.

Ruin gardens are one of the few places where the class divisions of the Mid City soften slightly. A merchant and a craftsman sit at the same table in a way that wouldn't happen in a formal inn. Nobody owns a ruin garden in any formally registered sense — operated by whoever installed the benches and the tap, tolerated by House Briganar as long as the tax on the ale is paid, treated by the neighbourhood as a communal resource whose legitimacy is entirely social.

The youth use them differently from the adults — the flat foundation stones as gathering places after dark, the wall sections as surfaces for things that get painted over by morning.

### THE SMALL CHURCHES

Each Mid City district clusters around a neighbourhood Mortalis church — smaller than the Cathedral, a single nave and one tower rather than two, the fist rose window above the entrance reproduced in miniature. These are maintained by parish workers rather than Gospellers — the known, unmasked faces the neighbourhood actually has opinions about. The community well is usually adjacent. The church functions as the district's administrative and social anchor in a way the Cathedral, too large and too institutional, cannot provide.

The parish workers here know everyone in a three-street radius. Who is behind on the Gospel tithe. Who has been sick. Whose children have stopped attending the morning literacy sessions. Whose household has had unfamiliar visitors. They do not report everything to House Bituris. They report what they calculate is worth reporting — and the calculation is made by people who have to live among the people they might report.

### STREET FURNITURE

**Iron anchor rings** sit flush into the flagstone of every market square — permanent fixtures, unobtrusive, purposeless six days a week. On Saturday morning the market traders arrive before dawn and the square transforms around these fixed points. Ropes and poles slot into rings and brackets, awnings go up, the entire market infrastructure assembles itself from what appears to be empty stone. By the time the Outer Ring children arrive mid-morning it looks like it was always there.

**Corner guards** protect the limestone building corners at street level — iron spurs or angled stone brackets preventing cart damage. In the Mid City these are maintained and, on the older streets nearest the ravine, decorated with Imperial pattern-work: geometric bands, the occasional palm-and-fist motif in low relief. A small piece of Imperial vocabulary at knee height, noticed mostly by children and people who have fallen over.

In the older sections near where Pre-Fall infrastructure sits closest to the surface, some corner guards are visibly ancient — the iron rusted dark, the stone beneath a different grade from everything around it. The decorative work on some of these is not Imperial. The figures are worn to near-illegibility by centuries of cart impact and deliberate grinding, but the shape of what used to be there is visible at the right angle in the right light. House Bituris has a ledger entry about them. The entry is old enough that no one has acted on it.

**Stone benches** are built into the base of certain walls — not furniture placed against stone but benches literally carved from the wall's lower course. In the Mid City they survive in the main thoroughfares and around market squares, occupied by the same elderly residents at the same hours every day with the reliability of civic infrastructure. In the Outer Ring most have been demolished for space, or worn to shin-height stumps that six people still somehow use out of accumulated habit.

**Public notice boards** appear at major junctions — stone-framed panels where House Briganar's local agents pin administrative notices, market prices, wanted notices, and occasionally things that should not be there and are removed before morning. Most of the Outer Ring population cannot read them. The Mid City population can, which is partly why Mid City residents are better informed about Imperial policy and partly why House Briganar controls the schools.

**Inn signs** are the one significant source of colour in the Mid City streetscape — wrought iron brackets extending from the facade, painted wooden boards identifying the establishment for those who cannot read the name below. Against the grey limestone and black ironwork, a well-painted sign is genuinely striking. The sign painters are a specific and reasonably well-compensated craft.

### EDUCATION

**The Outer Ring** — Gospel schooling, run by parish workers in the neighbourhood churches and any space commandeered for a few hours each morning. Teaching reading and basic numeracy to children whose families can spare them, which is not all families and not all mornings. The curriculum is narrow: enough literacy to read a notice board and a contract, enough numeracy for basic commerce, a substantial proportion of Gospel of Man doctrine delivered by people who genuinely believe it. For many Outer Ring children the parish workers are the primary institutional authority in their lives. House Bituris understands this perfectly.

Nobody is taught to read the Pre-Fall inscriptions that sit on surfaces throughout the city. This is not part of any curriculum anywhere.

**The Mid City** — proper schools closer to the Roman *ludus* tradition, run by educated administrators or retired clerics, teaching a broader curriculum. Reading, numeracy, official history, some natural philosophy. House Briganar recruits clerical staff from their graduates and has a quiet institutional interest in school quality. Some are Gospel-adjacent. Some are purely secular. Wealthier families pay for private tutors on top, covering languages and account-keeping — the education that produces factors and administrators rather than craftsmen.

The gap between what Outer Ring and Mid City children learn is one of the most durable mechanisms of social stratification in Antitheis. It is not enforced by law. It simply persists.

### THE SATURDAY MARKET

One day a week the Mid City opens. The iron anchor rings receive their ropes before dawn. The covered market squares fill with stalls extending outward along the arcade streets. The boundary between Mid City commerce and what comes in from outside becomes genuinely permeable.

The Outer Ring traders occupy the outer edges — cheaper goods, louder selling, the specific energy of people who know they are guests in someone else's territory and are performing accordingly. The established Mid City traders hold the central positions. The ruin gardens do more business in a single Saturday than in the rest of the week combined.

The children from the Outer Ring and the undercity come for reasons that have nothing to do with commerce. For a child who grew up in the deep Outer Ring where the sky is a strip and everything smells of smoke and bodies, the Mid City on a Saturday is genuinely overwhelming — the light, the space, the smell of actual food being prepared. The undercity children navigate by the temperature gradient: follow the warmth upward until it gets cool and breezy, then keep going until the noise increases. That is the market.

They are not entirely welcome and they know it. The Mid City traders watch their stalls. The parish workers watch the children. But the market is too large and too busy for effective exclusion, and the children are fast and know how to be invisible in crowds, and they come every week regardless.

### CATUVAR — THE MUSTER

The board game of Ath. Played on a 9x9 grid — the odd number gives a true centre square with no natural opposite, which experienced players fight over from the first move. Pieces are carved from two contrasting materials; in the Mid City, pale limestone and dark basalt. In the Outer Ring, whatever is available — two grades of worn stone, or bone.

**Each player commands:**
- **1 Warlord** — moves one square in any direction. Capturing it ends the game immediately, no announcement of threat required.
- **3 Captains** — move any number of squares in a straight line. Cannot jump pieces.
- **1 Scout** — moves in an L-shape, two squares one direction and one perpendicular. The only piece that can jump. Begins on the outermost column.
- **5 Soldiers** — move one square forward, capture one square diagonally forward. Reaching the opponent's back rank promotes them to Captain.

**Starting arrangement:** The Warlord occupies column five of the back rank. Captains sit on columns three and seven. The Scout occupies column one. The five Soldiers fill the second rank at columns two, four, five, six, and eight — irregular spacing, the Warlord protected directly ahead but with gaps on either side.

**The Sack:** Once per game a player may sacrifice one of their own pieces voluntarily — removing it without capturing anything — in exchange for an immediate additional move. The Sack cannot be used if the bonus move would directly capture the opponent's Warlord. It can threaten the Warlord. It cannot take it in the same sequence.

In formal rules documents the manoeuvre is called the sacrifice. In Antitheis street play it has acquired a different name. Players call it the Excision. Nobody has officially noted the connection.

### THE RAVINE DISTRICT

The ravine district is the most vertically complex part of Antitheis and the most difficult to understand without walking it. A map shows a gap in the city where the river cuts through. What the map cannot show is the five distinct levels of settlement stacked within that gap, each with its own character, its own population, its own relationship to the sky above and the water below.

Sound behaves differently in the gorge. The walls reflect and amplify in ways that make the district louder than its population would suggest — the noise of the gorge floor rises and bounces off the cliff faces and arrives at the upper levels distorted and delayed, so that people in the terraced mid-levels hear an echo of the waterfront activity several seconds after it happens, like the city is talking to itself. In winter the gorge funnels wind in a specific direction that the residents learn to anticipate. In summer it traps heat at the floor and creates its own weather system — mist rising off the river on cold mornings, the particular quality of air that exists only in enclosed wet stone spaces.

The gorge walls are not blank stone. They have been worked, carved, cut into, extended, painted, stained, and covered with two centuries of accumulated human modification. The oldest Pre-Fall stonework at the base shows in the foundations of the terrace buildings and in the carved channels that originally managed the river's seasonal variation. Above that, the Rebuilding period's more pragmatic work. Above that, the organic accretion of everything since. The colour of the gorge walls changes as you move through the district — the dark natural limestone at the base, then the ochre and rust and deep blue-green of the dye works' permanent staining on the cave district walls, then the lighter grey of the terrace buildings' stone, then the decorated facades of the upper ravine where the district meets the Mid City.

**Level One — The Gorge Floor**

The floor of the gorge is working ground. The river runs through its channelled bed, wide enough in the central section for two loaded barges to pass with some argument, with stone-faced docking areas on both banks where goods arrive and depart at any hour the water permits. The light here is partial at best — the gorge walls rise steeply on both sides and the direct sun reaches the floor only in the middle hours of summer days. The rest of the time the gorge floor operates in a grey, reflected, underwater quality of light that the workers who live and labour there stop noticing within a few weeks of arrival.

The barracks line the gorge walls at the floor level — long, low buildings pressed directly against the cliff face, built into the rock where the rock permitted, built against it where it did not. They house the dock workers who need to be present when boats arrive, which is determined by the river's tidal rhythms and the barge captains' schedules rather than any civic clock. The barracks culture is accordingly unusual — a shift-based community that sleeps in rotation and eats at hours that have no relationship to the dusk bell above. The residents are tight-knit in the way that people sharing confined quarters and unusual schedules always become tight-knit. They know each other's names, rotations, temperaments, and problems. They do not necessarily know much about the city above the gorge lip.

At the very edges of the gorge floor — the narrow strips where the cliff meets the water before any docking or building has been possible — waste accumulates. Industrial runoff from the cave district above, the river's own deposits, the refuse of the working community. These alcoves are the gorge floor's least considered spaces, dark even at midday, the air in them carrying the specific quality that the long-term barracks workers have learned to avoid without being able to say precisely why. Blaedre's presence in the gorge is strongest here. The water that pools in the deepest alcoves does not smell like river water. Nobody drinks it. Nobody has needed to be told.

**Levels Two and Three — The Cave District**

Above the gorge floor, carved into the natural limestone of the cliff face or enlarged from natural fissures, the cave district is the ravine's industrial core. The caves maintain a consistent temperature year-round — cool in summer, warmer than the exposed air in winter — which makes them valuable for specific purposes. Dyers work here because the humidity is exactly what certain dyeing processes require. The reds and blues and deep greens that have stained the cave district's walls over two centuries of this work are permanent, the stone having absorbed the colours into its surface so thoroughly that the cliff face here reads from the opposite bank as a geological phenomenon rather than the record of human industry it actually is. Coopers, tanners, certain food preservation operations that benefit from consistent cool — all of these concentrate in the deeper cave spaces where the temperature advantage is greatest.

The outer caves — shallower, lighter, with better ventilation — are temporary storage. Goods that have arrived by boat and are waiting for collection, or goods moving between levels of the city that need a staging point. The cave storage infrastructure is managed by an informal but deeply established network of factors whose family operations have controlled specific cave spaces for generations. The formal registration of these arrangements with House Briganar is partial and selective; the actual territorial understanding among the cave factors is comprehensive and self-enforcing.

As the caves give way to the cliff face proper, the cave district transitions to residential construction — buildings that use the cliff as a rear wall, the rock face serving structural functions that dramatically reduce the building material required. These residences are among the most comfortable in the ravine district. Consistent temperature, quiet from the thermal mass of the stone, genuinely sheltered from the gorge's weather. The people who have managed to establish themselves in cliff-face residences have no particular desire to move.

**Level Four — The Tailor's Bridge**

The oldest bridge crossing the ravine has been continuously inhabited for over a century, and what it has become in that time is not what anyone planned. It began as a structural accommodation — buildings added to the bridge deck to use available space, then more buildings, then the bridge so thoroughly built over that the crossing itself became a street rather than a span, and the street became a district, and the district became the most visually distinctive concentrated space in Antitheis.

The textile and fashion trade concentrated here through a logic that is now so established it seems inevitable but was originally contingent on the bridge's specific geography. Bolts of cloth arrive by boat at the gorge floor, pass beneath the bridge through a low stone arch — the underpass is precisely high enough for a loaded barge at normal water levels and the boatmen who navigate it have the passage memorised — and are unloaded at the bridge's foundation-level staging area where the dyers and weavers can take direct delivery without the goods ever having to be carried up through the city above. The supply chain runs vertically rather than horizontally, which in a city built on vertical geography is the most efficient possible arrangement.

The bridge workshops are therefore stocked with materials that have come directly from the boats below, treated in the cave district's dye works, and worked into finished and semi-finished goods that move upward to the Mid City studios and showrooms, or are sold directly from the bridge's own commercial premises. Every part of the textile production process exists in the ravine district, and the bridge is where it culminates. The workshops open onto the bridge deck itself — wide arched openings allowing the work to be conducted in public view, bolts of finished cloth hung from the window brackets as display and as signal that the workshop is open and the work is good.

The bridge people are proud in a way that requires no justification and therefore needs no defence. They are not quite the ravine district below and not quite the Mid City above. They are the bridge, which is a specific identity that two centuries of inhabitation have made as distinct as any district in the city. They take from both worlds and present themselves through both vocabularies simultaneously — the working practicality of the gorge district expressed through the visual sophistication of the fashion trade, producing a character that is uniquely theirs.

The bridge is where you go in Antitheis to be seen. Not to be seen in the upper city sense of social positioning through coded signals, but to be seen in the more direct sense of presenting yourself and having people actually look. The fashion here is more expressive and less governed by the subtle hierarchies of upper city dress — a journeyman dyer wearing something spectacular that a minor noble would be unwilling to risk. The colour is the point. The cut is the point. The bridge's own fashion culture generates trends that flow both upward toward the upper city and outward toward the Mid City, arriving in each place already slightly transformed by the journey.

Somewhere in the middle section of the bridge, above the underpass where the cloth boats arrive, a workroom and storage space operates under the name of a small textile consultancy. The workroom is Kira Carantis's. Not her studio — that is in the Mid City proper, her professional address, where her clients come — but where she actually works when she needs to work without interruption, surrounded by fabric rather than commerce. The bridge craftspeople know her. The bridge has known her for long enough that the question of how long has stopped being interesting. She sources materials here, keeps relationships with specific suppliers whose judgment she trusts, and occasionally does work here that the studio's schedule would not otherwise accommodate. The bridge does not particularly care about the name Carantis in any political sense. It cares that she knows cloth and pays fairly and does not condescend.

**Level Five — The Upper Ravine**

The fifth level is the transition — where the ravine district's terrace buildings approach the gorge lip and their upper floors become indistinguishable from the lowest level of the Mid City proper. The buildings here are the most prosperous of the ravine district and the least prosperous of the Mid City, and the people who live in them occupy both identities simultaneously depending on which conversation they are in. The facades on the gorge-facing side carry the decorative aspiration of the ravine district — arched windows, ironwork, carved lintels, the display that the visibility from across the gorge has historically incentivised. The facades on the street side are Mid City stone, matter-of-fact, registered with House Briganar in the standard format.

This is where the ruin gardens begin — the upper ravine's partial exposures of Pre-Fall stonework that were too expensive to demolish and too irregular to build over, converted into the drinking and gathering spaces we have already described. The Pre-Fall walls here show their full height in places in a way they do not in the Mid City proper, because the ravine's geology has preserved them where the flat city's development would long since have removed them. An afternoon in the upper ravine ruin gardens includes, if you look carefully, evidence of a city that neither the ravine below nor the Mid City above fully understands.

### THE TEMPERATURE GRADIENT

Antitheis has a distinct vertical temperature logic that shapes how different parts of the city are used across the seasons.

The ravine and undercity are the warmest zones year-round. Sheltered from wind by the gorge walls, surrounded by stone that absorbs heat during the day and radiates it through the night, with the river adding persistent humidity — the lower town stays warmer in winter and considerably hotter in summer than anything above it. The Undermarket tunnels below are the most extreme version: constant temperature, constant humidity, insulated from seasonal variation by the mass of stone and earth above. Practically useful for certain storage and craft purposes. Genuinely oppressive for extended habitation.

The Mid City on the ravine slopes sits in between — moderate airflow from the gorge draft, better light, the most comfortable zone through most of the year. The ruin gardens benefit particularly from this: the Pre-Fall walls create sheltered pockets that catch afternoon sun without the trapped heat of fully enclosed Outer Ring passages.

The Outer Ring on flat ground traps heat between densely packed buildings in summer. The smoke, the bodies, the stone holding the day's warmth through the night. In summer the deep Outer Ring passages are hot, still, and airless. The roofscape culture makes complete physical sense in this context — the rooftops are where the air moves.

The upper city and the Seat on high ground are the coolest and most wind-exposed. In summer the most desirable position. In winter, when the plateau winds come down from the Aldare direction, genuinely harsh. The wealthy maintain residences at different elevations and move between them seasonally — downhill in winter toward the warmth of the ravine district, uphill in summer to catch the breeze.

This seasonal movement has a social pattern attached. The lower-ravine districts become more socially mixed in winter as the wealthy move down. The upper city quiets. The Outer Ring never changes — always too hot in summer and inadequately heated in winter, and the people who live there have nowhere else to go.

## ENTERTAINMENT AND CULTURE IN ANTITHEIS

### THE VERTICAL CITY AND WHAT IT MEANS

There is a pattern to how people in Antitheis move for pleasure that mirrors almost exactly how they move through the social order. The Outer Ring children climb upward — to the roofscape, to the ridge runs, to the places where the air moves and nobody owns anything. The undercity people go downward — into the Pre-Fall tunnels, into the spaces beneath the officially recorded city, into the dark where nobody can observe them. The Mid City children climb the Pre-Fall ruins because the ruins are neutral territory, belonging to no social tier. The adults who have arrived at the Mid City and stayed there move neither up nor down — they sit in the ruin gardens at street level and drink ale, which is its own kind of statement about having reached the place they were aiming for.

The people who don't need to move vertically are the ones who already occupy the right level. This is not a coincidence. The city's physical geography has produced, over two centuries, a perfect spatial encoding of its class structure. Nobody planned this. It simply accumulated.

### NIGHTBRASS — THE GARDEN MUSIC

Nightbrass emerged from a specific act of curiosity. Someone who worked near a Mortalis Cathedral — a maintainer's assistant, a persistent loiterer, a craftsman whose workshop backed onto the cathedral close — understood enough of the organ's mechanics to attempt reproduction. Not the full instrument, which requires knowledge and resources far beyond any individual's reach, but the principle: a tube of the right length produces a note, air forced through it consistently produces that note reliably enough to play.

The first Brass instruments were crude. Inconsistent in pitch, difficult to control, producing a sound somewhere between a horn and something nobody had heard before. Over a generation of iteration by people with enough metalworking knowledge to refine the tube and enough musical instinct to work out what to do with it, it improved. It never reached the mechanical sophistication of the organ because the full knowledge chain that produced the organ is partially lost — the current maintainers work from incomplete inheritance. What the Brass became instead is something rawer and more personal than the organ ever was. No two players' instruments are quite identically tuned, which produces a slightly detuned warmth when two Brass players perform together that has become characteristic of the style.

Nightbrass as an evening tradition is built around three instruments: the Brass as the lead melodic voice, a plucked string instrument providing a quiet rhythmic pulse underneath, and light hand percussion keeping time loosely enough that it feels like a suggestion rather than a structure. The three together produce something that sits comfortably beneath conversation on a warm evening in a ruin garden — present when the crowd quiets, receding when the crowd is loud, following the people's ebb and flow. A good Nightbrass group reads the evening and responds to it. The pieces are long and don't really end so much as pause.

The melodies are modal and repetitive — structures that build through accumulation rather than development, the Brass player finding phrases and returning to them with small variations. The kind of melody that appears in your head two days later without your knowing how it got there. There's only a gentle smattering of applause after each piece. It aims to be a part of the atmosphere of the evening, like the temperature and the smell of the ale, rather than the focal point.

The local groups that play the ruin garden circuit are known by name within their district. Their sound is slightly different from the travelling performers who come for the Saturday market, who play things nobody has heard and leave before anyone can fully learn them. The local group plays the songs everyone knows. Sometimes that is exactly what an evening needs.

### DOCKBEAT — THE PIT MUSIC

Dockbeat is older than Nightbrass by at least a century and makes no attempt to be subtle about what it is. It emerged from the waterfront — the rhythm of hauling rope and loading cargo, the need for music that could be heard over wind and water and shouting, the specific way that physical collective work generates physical collective sound. The drum and the horn are the instruments because they are the loudest instruments available and because the dock workers already had them.

The songs of the Dockbeat tradition are old enough that their origins are unclear even to the people who sing them. They are built around the circumlocutory storytelling tradition — the art of speaking around what cannot be named — and woven so thoroughly with oblique references and understood gaps that even House Bituris has difficulty establishing what exactly is being said. It is not worship. It is not obviously anything that the Excision doctrine specifically prohibits. It is just the songs that go with the work, and the work has been happening for two centuries, and the songs have been evolving the whole time to stay just inside the boundary of what can be permitted.

At the docks the music is communal and functional — it regulates collective effort, it marks the rhythm of shared labour, it is the soundtrack of people who have to be there. In the Pits it becomes something else.

### THE PITS

The Pits are what the Outer Ring calls its dive bars — low-ceilinged spaces in the older buildings, in the sections of the district where House Briganar's records are thinnest and official oversight is lightest. They are not registered for the purpose they serve. They operate in the grey space between what is formally sanctioned and what anyone is willing to spend enforcement resources on.

Inside a Pit the Dockbeat is played at a volume that makes conversation impossible, which is partly the point. The drum dominates — multiple drummers in the better-established Pits, the rhythm building until the room feels it in the chest and the floor. The horn cuts over the top. The people who come here are not coming to relax. They are coming to feel something loudly in a dark room with other people who also need to feel something loudly. This is a specific and genuine human need and the Pits serve it without apology.

The parish workers have made their calculation about the Pits and chosen not to act — or more precisely, chosen to act selectively, against the ones that become too prominent or too clearly associated with something beyond music. House Bituris watches the pattern of attendance more than the content of the music. What people do after they leave a Pit, who they meet, where they go — that is the information worth having.

The music in the Pits carries more of the old content than almost anything else in the public soundscape. The people who go there know this in the way you know something without being able to state it. The songs feel older than they should. The specific way certain phrases are shaped around their absent subjects produces an emotional response that has nothing to do with the official meaning of the words. Nobody discusses this. It is simply understood.

### THE LOWS

Where the Outer Ring meets the river canyon, the nightlife does not spread outward but downward. The Lows are a strip of drinking houses built into the canyon wall itself, terrace below terrace, each landing lit and loud, joined by stairs cut into the rock and railings worn smooth by decades of hands. The higher terraces are respectable enough — where a mid-city clerk or a pair of young professionals might come for a drink they can still talk over, out slightly too late on a night that had no business being late. The further down you go, the louder, the younger, the cheaper the drink, and the looser the rules. A descent through the Lows is a descent through the city's own sorting, played out over a single evening on a single stair.

At the very bottom — the lowest of the Lows, and the name the whole descent is really built around — is The Lowest of the Lows. A cavernous Dockbeat club set against the black water, the drums coming up through the stone floor before you have found the door, and a crowd that is everyone the city keeps apart everywhere else: Outer Ring and mid-city, dock hands and Collegium students, the sons of engineers and the sons of nobody, all of them young and all of them there for the same reason. It is the one place in Antitheis where the vertical sorting that governs everything else simply stops, because at the bottom of the canyon there is nowhere lower left to put anyone. People talk about it the way people talk about a rite of passage. *You went down the Lows.* It means something to have been.

### THE SATURDAY PERFORMERS

The travelling performers who come for the Saturday market and stay through the week if they can afford to represent the most watched and the most creative end of Antitheis's entertainment culture. Acrobats, musicians, storytellers, animal handlers, people whose specific skills become entertainment in the right context. They occupy the most informal end of the Mid City economy and the most carefully observed end of House Bituris's attention.

The storytellers among them are the most significant. The Viriman circumlocutory tradition — the art of narrating the entire arc of what cannot be named without naming it — reaches its highest technical accomplishment in the travelling storyteller who has performed the same material in a hundred different cities and refined every gap and every oblique phrase to its most affecting form. A skilled travelling storyteller can reduce a ruin garden to complete silence with a story about a sleeper who dreamed and a man who woke him, in which neither the sleeper nor the man is ever named, and in which nothing divine is ever directly invoked. The silence after is the response. Nobody applauds this kind of performance either.

The musicians who travel are different from the Nightbrass garden groups — they play things nobody has heard before, combining instruments in unexpected ways, sometimes incorporating sounds from other continents entirely. They leave before anyone can fully learn what they were doing, which is part of why people talk about them for months afterward.

### THE GAMBLING DEN

In the older Pre-Fall ruins of the Mid City, beneath the foundation level that the current city was built on, there are rooms. Stairs going down that the current population didn't construct and doesn't fully understand. Spaces that exist on no House Briganar record because they predate the registration system entirely.

One of the more accessible of these — accessible meaning known to enough people that it functions as an institution, not accessible meaning easy to find — houses a gambling operation of some significance. The stakes at the Catuvar tables here are real. Debts accrue and are collected by people whose professional interest in collection is serious. The space is warm year-round, insulated by the Pre-Fall stone, and entirely invisible from the surface city. Nobody who runs it appears on any official record in connection with it. The protection chain from the den to whatever Mid City administrative structure benefits from its existence is long enough that the connection cannot be established without effort nobody is willing to spend.

The Outer Ring children who make their way to the Mid City on Saturdays and find the ruin gardens are not meant to find the stairs. The ones who are curious enough and careful enough sometimes do anyway. Some of them come back. Some of them find a reason to come back regularly, which is where certain careers in the informal economy begin.

### CHILDREN AND PLAY

Mid City children have something the Outer Ring children don't — actual horizontal space. The market square, the ruin gardens, the courtyard behind the townhouse. Their play reflects this: chasing games, territorial games, games that require a defined area and the ability to run in a straight line. Where Outer Ring children play in vertical space because horizontal space doesn't exist, Mid City children play on the ground because the ground is available to them.

Catuvar is learned young, played on chalk-scratched grids on flagstone. The pieces are carved from scraps — a Mid City child with a craftsman parent has access to offcuts of stone or bone that the Outer Ring child doesn't. Informal tournaments happen in the market square on Saturday afternoons, children watching adult games and learning by watching, which is how it has always been learned.

The Pre-Fall ruins are climbing structures. The exposed foundation stones are territories in games whose rules are completely opaque to any adult watching. The vines that grow over the upper sections are pulled at and swung from until someone in authority notices. The children who live in the ravine-facing districts have their own specific entertainment in the gorge — watching the boat traffic, fishing from the lower bridges, the specific fascination with depth that children near any significant drop tend to develop. Falling in is a genuine risk and a genuine dare, and the combination of those two things is sufficient explanation for why it happens regularly.

### THE BATH HOUSE

The primary social institution for adult Mid City leisure — not primarily hygiene but the extended conversation that happens in warm water with nowhere else to be. Business discussed, gossip exchanged, informal deal-making that doesn't happen at formal counters. The bath house keeper knows everything about everyone in a three-street radius, and unlike the parish workers has no obligation to share any of it with House Bituris, which makes them a specific and valuable kind of confidant.

The better Mid City bath houses also host organised Catuvar matches with stakes that matter — real money, debts, occasionally things other than money. House Briganar technically regulates gambling. The Catuvar betting culture in bath houses operates in a grey area that nobody finds worth closing, partly because several of the people who would have to close it are regular players.

## DRESS AND FASHION IN ANTITHEIS

*A note before beginning: what people wear in Antitheis is a language. Not a metaphor for a language — an actual system of communication with grammar and vocabulary that every resident learns to read before they fully understand what they are reading. The house colours of the upper city, the material quality of a Mid City wool coat, the specific dark practicality of the counter-culture youth, the expressive riot of the fashion bridge — all of these are statements, made continuously, by everyone, about who they are and who they are trying to be. Krad Kraps*

### THE BASE GARMENTS

Everyone in Antitheis wears a tunic. The specific form varies enormously by social position and purpose, but the underlying garment — torso covering, belted at the waist, falling to some point between the knee and the ankle — is the foundation of dress at every level of the city. The Gonelle is the standard everyday version: relatively plain, cut to allow movement, worn by the dock worker and the minor house administrator alike, differentiated only by the material it is made from and the quality of the belt worn over it.

The belt is not an afterthought. It is the garment's most personal element, the one item of dress that people across every social level invest in according to their means — the dock worker's worn leather strap with a good iron buckle, the Mid City factor's tooled leather with a brass fitting, the upper city functionary's embossed and chased piece that costs more than the garment it cinches. A person's belt tells you more about their self-conception than almost any other item they wear, because it is the one element that persists through different outfits and different occasions.

Beneath the tunic, hose — wool in cold weather, linen in warm, the quality and fit ranging from the deliberately fitted hosiery of the upper city to the loose and practical legwear of the Outer Ring. Boots for labour and outdoor work, shoes for less physically intensive occasions. The Outer Ring favours heavy practical boots that last. The Mid City favours a middle ground. The upper city's footwear is where the most deliberate craft goes — the shoemakers of the fashion bridge do their best work in shoes rather than boots, the cobbler's art visible in the last and the finishing.

### MATERIALS AND THE SOCIAL HIERARCHY OF CLOTH

The material of a garment places its wearer in the social order more precisely than the garment's cut or colour.

**Flax and linen** are the Outer Ring's primary cloth. Hardy, practical, relatively cheap, washable. Undyed linen is cream or off-white — the natural colour of the cloth — and most Outer Ring everyday wear is this colour simply because dyeing costs money. A well-made linen tunic is not shameful. It is honest. The Outer Ring has a cultural relationship to its working clothes that is matter-of-fact rather than aspirational — you wear what works and you keep it clean as long as the city permits.

**Wool** begins in Ostavar and arrives through the Interior trade routes, making it more expensive than local linen and therefore a social step up. Mid City wear is primarily wool — warmer, dyes more richly, holds its shape better than linen through daily use. The quality of the wool is the marker here: coarse wool is still working-class, fine Ostvari wool in a good colour is Mid City prosperity.

**Cotton** exists but is imported and inconsistent. The Mid City can afford it for specific garments but it is not the everyday base cloth here. The Outer Ring encounters it rarely and treats it carefully when it does.

**Silk** is upper city. Its presence in a garment signals access to the Gulf trade routes and the disposable income to spend on a fabric whose beauty has nothing to do with practicality. The upper city's silk comes through House Yambal's commercial networks and is priced accordingly.

**The deep indigo** of Imperial rank is a dye rather than a fabric — it can be applied to wool, silk, or fine linen — but its specific shade, derived from the shellfish processed on the Gulf Coast, is expensive enough to function as a material in social terms. The gradations of this indigo encode rank with precision that requires knowledge to read. The wrong shade worn by the wrong person is noticed.

### EVERYDAY DRESS BY DISTRICT

**The Outer Ring** wears linen tunics, practical hose, boots. Colour is limited — natural cream and off-white, occasional plant-dyed earth tones for those who can afford the small luxury. The belt is the personality. Women add length to the tunic, sometimes a wrapped cloth at the head — a **kerchief** or simple **coif** — as much for practical hair management as modesty.

Every Outer Ring family keeps a set of festival clothes separate from the working wardrobe. These are the garments that receive the care the everyday clothes do not — stored carefully, repaired precisely, worn only for the occasions that justify their wearing. The festival wardrobe is where colour appears in Outer Ring dress: a dyed wool overdress, a good belt, the boots polished rather than merely cleaned. The contrast between festival dress and everyday dress in the Outer Ring is a visual measure of what this population keeps sacred.

The counter-culture youth — the Outer Ring side of the Dreamers' orbit, those who move through the sewer networks and the night city — have converged on a practical aesthetic that is also a statement. Dark linen, dark wool, nothing that catches light or reads clearly from a distance. The hood worn low. The cuts chosen for movement rather than appearance. The anti-fashion is as deliberate as bridge fashion, just pointed in the opposite direction. When the Dreamers gather for meetings, its members add **tabards** over their everyday dress — simple rectangular garments worn over the head and hanging front and back, in a dark common colour. Not a uniform exactly. A signal that this gathering is the thing rather than any individual in it.

**The Mid City** has more room for choice and exercises it. The Gonelle gives way to more varied silhouettes — a fitted **cotehardie** for those who want to show shape, a looser **surcoat** for those who prefer ease, the **lúmann** draped over the shoulders for outdoor movement and for the specific Mid City social register of dressed without being formal. The lúmann is old — Gaelic in origin, predating the Empire — and carries a quality of cultural continuity that the Mid City values. It says: I am not trying to impress anyone with my modernity. It is also, in good wool, genuinely elegant.

For headwear the Mid City favours **hoods** for practical weather management, **scapulars** — simple rectangular over-garments that cover the shoulders and fall front and back — for the slightly more formal occasions that the Mid City produces regularly, and the **baghat** for everyone else. The baghat is a soft draped cap — wool or linen, folded or bagged over the head with no rigid structure, comfortable and slightly characterful depending on how the wearer chooses to fold it. Not a status symbol. A personal choice made daily. The colour of someone's baghat is often the most individual thing about their appearance.

Women in the Mid City add **cowls** — soft draped hood-and-collar combinations — as the standard alternative to the open-head options favoured by men. **Kerchiefs** continue from the Outer Ring but in better material and more deliberately arranged.

**The upper city** dress is what everything below it is measured against and is itself measured against the house vocabularies that dominate it.

### HOUSE DRESS AND THE UPPER CITY VOCABULARY

The five great houses dress their members in consistent colour vocabularies that function as institutional uniforms while maintaining the fiction of personal choice. Everyone in the upper city knows what each colour means. No one acknowledges that they know.

**House Catubrix** wears dark grey and deep ox-blood red. The primary garment is the **doublet** — fitted, structured, the body's shape contained and armoured by the cut — worn with a **half-cape** draped from one shoulder. The Bec de Corbin and shield emblem appears at the breast or on the cape clasp. There is nothing decorative about Catubrix dress that does not serve the structure. The silhouette says: contained force. The colour says: metal and what metal does. Even the civilian members of the house dress in a way that implies they could be soldiers if the situation required it.

**House Briganar** wears dark forest green. The **gabardine** is their characteristic garment — a long, slightly full outer coat, wool or fine wool, that moves with the wearer and allows for the long hours of sitting and document-handling that defines the house's work. The half-open scroll and feather pen emblem appears subtly, often embroidered at the cuff rather than displayed prominently. Briganar dress says: I am here to work and I am good at it. The forest green reads as old, as established, as a colour that was chosen by people who were already here.

**House Yambal** wears deep blue and purple — the galactic end of the spectrum, colours that edge close enough to the Imperial indigo to be interesting and differ from it enough to be distinct. The anchor emblem appears on their dress in forms that range from plain to extraordinarily worked depending on the occasion. Gulf wealth is visible in the quality of the material rather than the volume of the display. Yambal dress is the most beautiful in the upper city and the most conscious of being beautiful. The colours are also — and this is not discussed — the closest thing in contemporary dress to the visual vocabulary of the pre-Fall divine. Whether this is coincidence or private observance made public and deniable, the house's oldest members could say and do not.

**House Bituris** wears grey. Not one grey — shades of grey, multiple tones worn simultaneously, jacket and shirt and breeches and cloak all subtly different values of the same non-colour, the effect being a person who reads as present and unreadable simultaneously. The eye emblem appears on their dress in a placement that requires looking at the person directly to find — not prominently displayed but definitely there. Bituris members are the most unsettling dressed people in the upper city and they are unsettling in the specific way of something that has been thought about very carefully.

**House Ambarris** wears amber and wheat tones — warm, slightly golden, the colour of grain and good harvest and also of someone who has studied what a house colour should be and made a considered choice that reads as a considered choice. Their cartwheel emblem is displayed with the slight over-prominence of new money's new symbol. Ambarris members dress with the nervousness of people who have checked multiple times that everything is correct and are still not entirely sure it is. This is not uncharming. It is the most human house to look at.

### KIRA CARANTIS AND THE BRIDGE

Kira Carantis wears no house colours. In the upper city this reads as a statement; in the Mid City it simply reads as someone who is not affiliated, which is common enough. Her personal palette runs toward **deep reds** — burgundy, garnet, the darker side of red — and avoids orange entirely. She works primarily in formal dress: the structured garment for significant occasions, the piece that makes the person wearing it feel equal to what they are walking into.

Her particular skill is silhouette. She reads bodies and makes garments that work with what is actually there rather than imposing a fashion ideal onto it. A person who is pear-shaped leaves Kira's studio with something that celebrates the flow of their lower half rather than apologising for it. The emphasis goes where the body already draws the eye, reinforced rather than redirected. Her clients — the ones who understand what she actually does rather than simply wanting to be well-dressed — describe the experience of wearing her work as feeling like themselves on their best day.

The fashion bridge is where this tradition began and where it continues to be made. The bridge's culture of expression — the most visually demonstrative community in Antitheis, where self-presentation is practiced and valued as a craft in its own right — is also the incubator for most of the trends that eventually become the city's fashion. The upper city refines and codifies. The Mid City adopts and moderates. But the bridge is where something new appears first, worn by someone who made it themselves from materials that came off a boat the previous morning, seen by everyone who crosses between one bank and the other on their way to or from somewhere else.

Armlets and leglets — decorative metal bands worn at the upper arm or calf — appear on the bridge more than anywhere else in the city. Expensive relative to most Outer Ring budgets, occasionally affordable in the lower-quality versions for Mid City workers, and common enough on the bridge that they function as its specific decorative vocabulary. The bridge workers who have made good wear them. The ones who haven't wear them anyway, in whatever version they can manage, because on the bridge what you wear is what you say you are.

Any account of architecture on Ath must address the negative spaces — the places where things used to be that are no longer there, and what their absence looks like.

Iconoclasm leaves a specific physical record. It is not the same as destruction. Destruction removes the object entirely. Iconoclasm leaves the object present but emptied of meaning — ground smooth, whitewashed over, the support structure of what was believed remaining while the belief itself is removed. The result, in built form, is a world full of outlines.

The smooth patches on the old columns of the Inner Ward. The places on Pre-Fall stonework where the grinding was thorough enough to remove the image but not thorough enough to remove the memory of depth — a surface that is technically flat but catches the light differently from the original face of the stone, betraying the contours of what used to be there. The baptisteria of civic buildings with their dry basins and their entrance inscriptions that redirect the eye from what the space obviously used to do toward what the Empire now claims it is for.

In the northern villages, where the Excision's reach is less immediate, the iconoclasm is less complete. The fossilised rituals — the bread baked in the shape of a sleeping figure, the particular way certain crossroads are treated on certain nights — persist not because the Empire has chosen to permit them but because it has not chosen to prioritise addressing them. There are only so many Paradigm Fists. There are considerably more crossroads.

What is absent from Ath's built environment, and conspicuously so once one has learned to notice it, is any image of the sky that is not subordinate to something human. Other continents I have visited represent the heavens in their public buildings as something to aspire toward, to wonder at, to feel small beneath. On Ath, where the sky appears at all in public imagery, it is either absent from the frame, compressed into the background of a human scene, or in the case of the Mortalis Cathedrals' fist window, something being challenged. The collective built environment of the continent is the physical expression of a culture that decided, two centuries ago, to stop looking up.

I find myself wondering, on certain evenings in Antitheis when the light is right and the old columns catch it at the angle that reveals their ground-away surfaces, what those surfaces showed. What was considered important enough to carve into the stone. What someone decided, later, was important enough to remove.

I find myself wondering whether the removal was complete.

## THE SEWER CHILDREN

Every city produces children who find the spaces adults have decided not to think about, and Antitheis is no exception. The Pre-Fall tunnel network beneath the city — the passages that the Rebuilding period's drainage infrastructure incorporated partially and left partially alone — has been discovered independently by successive generations of Outer Ring children for as long as the tunnels have existed, and the culture that has accumulated around this discovery is by now as established as any other Outer Ring tradition.

The sewer children are not the Dreamers. They are not ideological. They are children who found a way underground and went, because underground is where adults are not, and the absence of adults is the primary thing a child in the Outer Ring's cage homes and crowded streets consistently seeks.

The culture that has developed around underground exploration is specifically competitive in the way that children's cultures always find their particular axis of status. The currency here is knowledge — who has memorised the most passages, who can navigate from one section to another without a lamp, who has mapped the deepest and most complex sections of the network in their head. The children who have been going down the longest carry their knowledge with the specific pride of expertise earned through genuine risk, and the newer arrivals who want to be taken further must prove themselves in the shallower sections first. Going into the most dangerous-looking passage is a mark of distinction. Coming back from it is the proof.

The entry points are known within specific age cohorts and not outside them — the loose grate in the alley behind the tannery, the broken section of drainage channel accessible from the ruin garden's lower terrace, the passage beneath the oldest section of the Outer Ring wall that connects to the Pre-Fall network proper. These locations pass from older children to younger ones as social currency. Adults occasionally find an entry point and have it repaired. Another one is found or made.

Inside, the passages are named in the oral tradition of the cohort — names that persist for years and then shift as the population of users changes. The dangerous sections are marked by methods that require knowing the code to read: a specific chalk mark, a stone placed in a certain way, an absence of the small personal tokens that children leave in the safer passages to mark their presence. The tokens accumulate in the best sections — small objects left not as tribute to anything but simply as evidence of having been there, the Pre-Fall stone surfaces acquiring a sediment of Outer Ring childhood.

### THE UNDERGROUND ECOLOGY

The sewer network is not empty of life. Beyond Blaedre's residual presence in the oldest and deepest channels — the wrong-smelling water, the specific quality of dread that the children learn to register without being able to name — the tunnels support a population of creatures that have adapted to the underground environment over generations.

The crocodiles that entered the system through the river connections in a period nobody now remembers are the most practically dangerous. They occupy the water-adjacent sections closest to the Onnoris and are known well enough within the sewer children's community to constitute a specific category of passage warning — not just dangerous-looking but crocodile territory, which is a different and more immediately fatal category. The tunnel gangs of the Undermarket maintain an informal truce with the crocodile populations in their operating areas, having learned that the alternative is worse. The creatures are not hunted because they are too dangerous and too useful as a deterrent against uninvited penetration of certain passages.

The swamp sections — where the drainage infrastructure has failed and the passage floors have accumulated standing water and the organic material that standing water in a tunnel produces — contain things that resist specific identification. The sewer children call them slimes or creep-crawlies and describe them in ways that the Collegium's biological science department, if shown the accounts, would find interesting. The things that live in the swamp sections are not aggressive unless disturbed. The homeless encampments that have established themselves in the drier upper passages maintain their distance from the swamp sections through the accumulated wisdom of people who have learned what disturbing them produces.

Flooding events reshape the underground ecology periodically. When Blaedre moves — in the way that a vast, ancient, barely-conscious thing moves, which is less a decision than a shift in the weight of something enormous — water travels through the tunnel network in a surge that the surface city above registers only as a sudden dramatic increase in drainage flow and an unusual smell from the Gospel wells. In the tunnels, the surge is catastrophic for anything that cannot move faster than the water. The homeless encampments post watchers in the sections where the water first rises when this happens. They have learned the signs. They have not always learned them in time.

### THE HOMELESS ENCAMPMENTS

The sewer network's drier upper sections — the Pre-Fall passages that run at a level above the active drainage infrastructure — support a population of Antitheis residents who have exhausted every other option the city offers. These are not people who chose the underground. They are people for whom the underground is what remained when everything above it was foreclosed.

The encampments are organised according to the logic of any community that has self-selected for resilience — the people who have been there longest hold the best-positioned spaces and maintain the internal rules that keep the encampment functional. The rules are practical: no fires in sections where the ventilation is insufficient, no attracting the attention of Undermarket gangs if avoidable, no going past certain passages alone, and the most recent addition — do not tell anyone above ground about what was found in the eastern network.

What was found in the eastern network has been kept between the encampment's longest-established residents for three years. They found it by following a light source that had no business existing underground, moved toward it because light underground means warmth and warmth is the most valuable commodity in the deep passages at night, and found themselves at an entrance they did not know existed. What they saw through it they have discussed among themselves in the specific terms of people trying to process something they have no framework for. They see shelter. They see light. They see a sky. They have not yet agreed on whether what they see justifies the risk of entering, or whether what they see is actually what it appears to be, or whether the thing that must have made it knows they have found the entrance. They have agreed, unanimously and without formal discussion, that they will not tell anyone. Not until they understand what they have.

### HORAMA OF THE INERT ARTIFICUM

There are paintings in Memories Caged — catalogued, filed, part of the permanent collection — by an artist whose name appears in the Golden Era records as one of the most celebrated realist painters of his generation. The name on the catalogue entries is a birth name that the subject no longer uses and that very few living people would connect to anything except those paintings. The works themselves are extraordinary: light rendered with a precision that makes the painted surface appear to have actual depth, texture so accurately observed that viewers have been documented reaching toward the canvas to confirm what their eyes are telling them. The Collegium's art history faculty considers them the finest surviving examples of pre-Fall realist technique.

The artist who made them has been in the sewers beneath Antitheis for longer than anyone above ground has been looking for him.

He is called Horama of the Inert Artificum. Horama because it is what he chose when he discarded the birth name, from an old word for sight and seeing. Inert Artificum because the title names what he works against — the crafted thing that does not live, the art that captures without animating, the representation that falls short of reality in the one way that matters. His entire underground project is a sustained argument with that insufficiency.

The belief energy that sustained his original career was genuine and accumulated over decades — the collective admiration of everyone who stood before his canvases and felt, for a moment, that the painted light was actual light. That accumulation gave him something beyond technical skill: a perception so developed it bordered on supernatural, an ability to see the qualities of surfaces and light that no other trained eye could access, and eventually, as the world that believed in him contracted and the belief energy began to starve, the desperate application of those accumulated capacities toward a different purpose entirely.

He discovered that what he could do with paint on canvas he could do, with sufficient development and experimentation, with paint on stone and air and space. The brush that once captured surfaces could, with the right intention and the right material, alter them. The skill that had rendered light now created it. The perception that had observed texture now imposed it. He built his light source first — a painted sun that produces actual warmth, whose quality of illumination reproduces the specific afternoon light of the city where he grew up. He painted the sky above his growing underground space second — not a fresco but something that behaves like sky, that carries weather within it, that produces the quality of being under open air in an enclosed underground space.

Under that painted sky he has been building his city for decades.

It is the city of his childhood — the one that existed before the Fall, the one that no longer exists above ground in any form. He is reconstructing it from memory and from the preserved materials his work requires. The streets are correct to within the margin of what memory and grief can produce. The buildings are structurally sound. The details are accurate to a period that nobody else alive can verify, which is possibly the point.

The inhabitants are his finest work and his most significant departure from his original practice. He found that paint and preservation could produce, with sufficient skill and sufficient belief, objects that were not quite alive and not quite dead — states he calls inert, which is the condition his whole project works against. He found that certain arrangements of preserved material, certain applications of his manipulated craft, could push the inert past its threshold into something that moved. Dogs that bark. Birds that fly short distances before returning to their frozen positions. Figures that walk their preserved routes through streets that no longer exist above ground.

He takes what he needs for his work from the city above — the city that forgot him, that lost the capacity to believe in him when it lost most of itself, that continued existing in its reduced form without the cultural infrastructure that his art had required. He does not take indiscriminately. He takes with the specific selectivity of an artist who knows what his next piece requires and acquires the material accordingly. The missing persons reports that surface in House Briganar's records every few months are attributed to the Undermarket's human trafficking operations, which is a reasonable attribution given everything else the Undermarket does and a convenient one given that investigating further would require going somewhere the official city has decided not to go.

The Outer Ring folklore about what lives underground and takes people is old enough and vague enough that it predates Horama by generations — the tunnels have always had their dangers, always had their missing children, always had the creep-crawlies and the monsters that parents invoke when their children are underground too long. Horama has simply given the folklore a new and very specific body without anyone above ground knowing it.

The Paradigm Fists do not know he exists. The Collegium knows only that a celebrated Golden Era painter disappeared during the Fall and that his surviving works are exceptional. The homeless encampment at the eastern network entrance knows something is in there and has not yet decided what to do about it. The sewer children know which passages not to go past and attribute this knowledge to Blaedre, which is partly wrong and partly functionally correct.

Somewhere under Antitheis, under a painted sky that produces real warmth, in the streets of a city that has not existed for two centuries, a figure is finishing something. The birds in this section are frozen at the moment of landing. The dogs are frozen mid-turn. The light is the specific quality of early afternoon in a place that nobody alive remembers. The artist considers the composition for a long time and then decides it needs one more element and picks up his brush and goes to find it.

## ANIMALS OF THE CITY

Antitheis sustains an animal population that no census has ever counted and no institution has ever formally claimed responsibility for, distributed through every district according to the logic of available food, available shelter, and available tolerance from the human population sharing the space.

**Working animals** are the most visible. The carriage horses of the Mid City — groomed, fed, stabled in the yards behind the for-hire establishments — are the city's most valuable and most carefully maintained animal population, their condition a direct reflection of the commercial interests they serve. The oxen that haul the heavy cargo at the river docks and the warehouse district are larger and less individually valued, their welfare managed at the level of the working asset rather than the prized possession. The donkeys that serve the Outer Ring's small-scale commerce — the vegetable seller's cart, the rag merchant's rounds — are somewhere between the two: worked hard, fed adequately, maintained at the level that keeps them functional.

The horses and oxen of the carriage trade produce significant quantities of waste that the city's drainage infrastructure was not originally designed to handle, and the management of this waste is one of House Ambarris's persistent administrative headaches. The night soil collectors handle human waste. Animal waste on the main thoroughfares is theoretically the responsibility of whoever owns the animal, which in practice means it is the responsibility of the municipal workers who clean the carriage lanes each morning before the day's traffic begins.

**Rats** are the Undermarket's most reliable permanent residents and the Outer Ring's most persistent problem. The population in the lowest and most food-adjacent sections of the city — the wet market at the waterfront, the warehouse district's less carefully managed storage, the cage home tenements where food is stored in conditions that rats find accommodating — is substantial enough to constitute a genuine public health concern that the parish medical facilities encounter regularly in the form of the bites and the fevers they produce. The cats that the Outer Ring keeps, semi-feral and operating on the implicit arrangement of shelter in exchange for hunting, are the most effective management tool available and are treated accordingly — not quite owned, not quite wild, fed enough to stay and not enough to stop hunting.

**Cats** exist across all districts in forms that reflect the district's character. The upper city cats are the most deliberately kept — household animals in the proper sense, fed from kitchens, permitted on furniture, occasionally the subject of the specific attention that wealthy people with sufficient leisure extend to animals that tolerate it. The Mid City cats are more varied, some kept and some tolerating domestic proximity without fully committing to it. The Outer Ring cats are functionally feral despite living in continuous proximity to humans, shaped by generations of selection for the hunting ability that justifies their presence rather than for temperament.

**Dogs** follow a similar gradient. The upper city breeds dogs with the specific purposefulness of people who have decided that a dog should have a function and have selected for it — the hunting dogs kept for the rare occasions when the upper city's residents travel to estates outside the walls, the guard animals at certain compound perimeters. The Outer Ring dogs are the city's most numerous and least controlled canine population, strays and semi-strays operating in loose territorial groups that the long-term Outer Ring residents have learned to read and navigate. They are not generally dangerous. They are not generally friendly. They are simply present in the way that all Outer Ring things that have found a sustainable niche are present.

**Pigeons** occupy the campanile stonework in numbers that the tower maintenance staff address every few years with varying success. The ledges and cavities of the Pre-Fall sections of the upper city wall support the largest urban raptor population in the city — the falcons that have nested in certain wall towers for generations, hunting the pigeons, known by name within the wall garrison community in the informal way that animals attached to specific places accumulate names over decades. The oldest nesting pair in the oldest Pre-Fall tower has had seventeen documented generations of offspring in the garrison's internal records, which began noting them for reasons that the records do not explain and continued out of accumulated habit.

**The fate-peering owls** of the campanile tops are a specific and documented phenomenon. The oldest owl currently resident in Senobel has been observed by multiple Collegium faculty members over seventeen years and is described in the Department of Immaterial Science's internal notes — not published, filed — as demonstrating perceptual responses to approaching individuals that cannot be accounted for by the owl's sensory capabilities alone. The owl does not predict futures. It notices things about the people who enter the tower that it should not be able to notice, and it responds to some of them in ways that the researchers recording this have described, carefully, as anomalous. The owl has not been captured or studied invasively. The researchers have agreed, without formally discussing their agreement, that this would be inadvisable.

**River birds** on the Onnoris — the herons that stand motionless in the shallows of the less industrialised bank sections, the various waterfowl that nest in the reeded margins north of the city wall, the kingfishers whose presence indicates water quality well enough that the dock workers treat their absence from certain sections as a practical warning — are part of the waterfront's ambient texture in the same way that the rats and cats are part of the Outer Ring's. You stop noticing them specifically and start noticing their absence when it occurs.

**Occasional arrivals** through the river ports from other continents are the city's most irregular animal encounters. The Gulf trade brings animals as cargo sometimes — livestock, exotic specimens for wealthy collectors, things that arrived in a ship's hold and were not originally intended as cargo. The port authority has a procedure for unusual animal arrivals that involves House Ambarris's commercial assessment and, for anything genuinely strange, a notification to the Collegium. The Steel Roses have a standing interest in these notifications and respond to them faster than the Collegium generally does.

## THE COLLEGIUM — A PHYSICAL ACCOUNT

The Collegium does not look like a single institution from outside it because it is not, architecturally, a single institution. It is a collection of buildings that have accumulated around a central administrative core over two centuries of expansion, connected to each other by a network of covered walkways and tunnels that makes the campus navigable to those who know it and genuinely disorienting to those who do not. The students who have been there longest wear their navigation knowledge with the specific ease of people who have earned it through weeks of being lost, and they offer directions to new arrivals with the measured generosity of people who remember exactly how useful a clear direction was when they needed one.

The campus sits on the elevated ground of the upper city, which means it occupies multiple levels of the hillside rather than spreading horizontally across a flat plane. Buildings are connected to buildings that are connected to buildings, some of the connections at the same grade and some requiring descent before ascent, the overall effect being that moving from the engineering workshops to the biological science department to the Conservatory traces a path that goes through three different elevations and two different tunnel sections and produces, in the process, three different qualities of light and temperature and acoustic atmosphere.

### THE BUILDINGS

The **main administrative hall** is the Collegium's formal face — the building whose entrance is visible from the street, whose facade has received the most deliberate architectural attention, whose great hall is used for the ceremonies that the institution presents to the outside world. It is Viriman civic grammar at its most practiced: thick limestone walls, rounded arches, the rotunda at its centre with the dome that draws the eye around rather than upward. The names of the Collegium's significant graduates are cut into the floor of the rotunda in the same spirit as the names in Once Lost Forever Found, though the selection criteria here reflect institutional pride rather than grief and the omissions are accordingly different in character.

The **lecture halls** occupy a separate building connected to the administrative hall by the main covered walkway — a long stone-arched passage open on the courtyard side, roofed against weather, its flagstone floor worn in the specific pattern of many feet taking the same route many times. The lecture halls themselves are tiered in the amphitheatre tradition, the seats rising from the demonstration floor where the teaching happens, the acoustics designed to carry a voice to the back row without effort. The chalk dust that accumulates on every surface in the lecture hall building is the institution's most characteristic smell — the specific dryness of ground mineral against stone, the underlying smell of the lamp oil that lights the windowless inner rooms, the occasional sharp note of whatever chemical preparation the natural philosophy faculty is using this week.

The **engineering workshops** are the loudest building on campus and are situated accordingly — as far from the residential colleges and the library as the campus geography permits, which is not as far as the neighbours would prefer. The specific percussion of the workshops in full operation — the mechanical testing equipment, the prototype construction, the occasional experiment that produces a sound the building was not designed to contain — is audible from the covered walkways on still days and has become part of the campus's ambient texture. The students who work in the engineering workshops have a specific social identity within the Collegium — more practically oriented, more comfortable with failure as a learning mechanism, more likely to be found in the workshops at hours when the rest of the campus has gone to bed.

The **biological science department** occupies a building that most of the campus visits only when they need to and does not linger in when they do not. The upper floors where the research happens have a smell that the building's ground floor attempts to contain with ventilation and does not fully succeed. The students who study here develop the specific professional distance from their material that we have described elsewhere, and they also develop, over time, a dark collegial humour that functions as the psychological infrastructure for doing what their work requires. The department has the highest rate of students arriving from outside the upper city — the scholarship students and the merchant families' children who are there specifically because the biological sciences represent a route into the medical profession that the upper city's private physician networks do not easily provide to outsiders.

The **Department of Immaterial Science** is the most deliberately inaccessible building on campus. It is reached by a specific tunnel — one of the older ones, possibly Pre-Fall in its lower sections — that branches from the main tunnel network in a direction that the campus maps mark clearly and that new students nonetheless miss regularly. The building at the end of this tunnel is smaller than its institutional significance suggests, its facade deliberately unremarkable, its windows fewer than the standard for a research building. The faculty who work here have offices that face inward rather than outward. The published research that emerges from the building is, as we have described, accurate and important and carefully titled to reveal as little as possible about what it actually measures. House Bituris's three faculty positions in this department are the worst-kept open secret on campus. Everyone knows. No one discusses it at faculty meetings.

The **Conservatory** is the campus's most acoustically specialised building and was designed with a specificity that distinguishes it from everything else on campus. The practice rooms are individually isolated, their walls thick enough that the student working through a difficult passage on the brass instrument cannot hear the student in the adjacent room working through a different difficult passage. The performance hall has a ceiling that was adjusted three times during construction to achieve the acoustic properties the founding faculty required. The Conservatory students occupy a social position on campus that is slightly separate from the academic departments — their schedule is different, their hours are different, their relationship to the campus's formal intellectual culture is different in ways that produce both mutual appreciation and mutual incomprehension.

### THE COVERED WALKWAYS AND TUNNELS

The covered walkways are where the informal Collegium happens. The formal Collegium happens in the lecture halls and the laboratories and the administrative offices. The real Collegium — the conversations that actually change how someone thinks, the arguments that continue for weeks, the friendships and enmities that persist for decades after graduation — happens in the walkways between classes, in the tunnels between buildings, in the specific social spaces that the campus's architecture creates without having designed them for that purpose.

The tunnels have their own character from the walkways. The walkways are open to the courtyard on one side and to weather, to light, to the visual context of the campus around them. The tunnels are enclosed, lamp-lit, acoustically intimate, separated from the outside world in a way that produces a different quality of conversation. Things said in the tunnels stay in the tunnels in the same way that things said in the bath house stay in the bath house — not by rule but by the social understanding that the tunnel creates a different register of privacy than the open walkway. The tunnel between the engineering workshops and the Department of Immaterial Science, in particular, has been the site of conversations that neither department would have wanted to have on the record, between students who were smart enough to make the connection between what each department was actually working on and had the sense to make it somewhere the connection could be denied.

### THE STUDENTS

The Collegium's student body is not homogeneous, and the social landscape that results from the mixing of upper city children, scholarship students from the Outer Ring, and the merchant families' children from the Gulf coast and the Interior is one of the most complex social environments in Antitheis.

The upper city children arrive with the specific social fluency of people who have been trained since birth to read and navigate hierarchies. They know the house affiliations, the family histories, the specific weight that a name carries. They form the cliques that the institution's social geography accommodates — the students connected to Catubrix aligning with the combat sports culture, the Briganar-adjacent students dominating the administrative and historical faculties, the Yambal-connected students bringing Gulf money and Gulf social confidence in combinations that the other groups find variously impressive and irritating.

The scholarship students from the Outer Ring are there because they are exceptional and they know it and they know that everyone else knows it and they navigate this with varying degrees of grace depending on their temperament. Some are absorbed into existing social groups through the mechanism of shared intellectual interest overcoming class origin. Some form their own community — the scholarship students across departments maintaining a lateral connection that crosses faculty lines in a way the upper city students' networks do not. Some remain individually isolated, present in the institution without being fully inside its social world, which is its own form of education in how institutions actually function.

The merchant families' children from the south and the wider Empire arrive with money that sometimes exceeds the upper city families' resources and social confidence that is calibrated for a different environment. They are not intimidated by wealth — they have wealth. They are navigating a social world whose specific codes they were not raised in, and the process of acquiring those codes while simultaneously making the academic case for their place at the institution produces a specific character type that the Collegium has seen in enough iterations to recognise immediately.

### THE SPORTS CULTURE

The Collegium's sporting culture reflects the institution's intellectual self-image — the understanding that competence in the physical disciplines, like competence in the academic ones, benefits from breadth of knowledge rather than narrow specialisation.

The **all-weapons combat programme** is the most prestigious sporting activity on campus and the one most directly connected to the upper city's duel culture. The principle is that a practitioner who understands every weapon is more dangerous and more sophisticated than one who has mastered only one, and the programme's curriculum moves through the full vocabulary of armed combat with the same systematic approach that the lecture halls apply to natural philosophy. The students who excel in this programme tend to come from Catubrix-connected families, where the martial tradition is domestic as well as academic. The occasional scholarship student from the Outer Ring who arrives with a different kind of physical education — the practical knowledge of actual violence rather than the formal study of controlled combat — finds that the programme's formal framework is not designed for what they already know, and that the adaptation runs in both directions.

The **rowing and swimming** programme takes the students out of the city to the Onnoris sections north of the walls, where the river is cleaner and the traffic is lighter. This is one of the few activities that requires leaving the campus and the upper city entirely, which the students who participate in it value as much as the athletic training itself — the specific relief of being somewhere that is not the Collegium's social world for a few hours, the river as a leveller in the same way the bath house is a leveller. The rowing competitions between student groups have developed their own elaborate tradition of ritual insult and counter-insult that is entirely internal to the programme and entirely incomprehensible to everyone outside it.

**Athletics** — running, jumping, throwing — happens on the terraced field below the main campus buildings, the slope of the upper city hillside having been cut into a series of level platforms that serve as the athletics ground. The view from the top of the athletics field, looking out over the Mid City and the Outer Ring toward the Onnoris, is one of the campus's incidental pleasures and is used by students who want to think rather than train as often as by students who want to train.

### THE BOULDERING SUBCULTURE

The least formal and most socially significant sporting activity at the Collegium has no official status, no faculty oversight, no competition structure, and no dedicated facilities. It happens in the ruin garden sections adjacent to the campus, in the Pre-Fall wall sections that provide climbing routes, and in the forested hillside approaches to the city where the rock faces exposed by road construction have been discovered by successive generations of students as superior to anything the campus itself offers.

The bouldering group is a subculture in the precise sense — a community with its own internal culture, its own knowledge base, its own social norms, operating within and around the institution without being defined by it. The membership overlaps with the Dreamers more than with any other campus group, which is not accidental — the activity takes people to Pre-Fall spaces, to old walls and old stone, to the places where the city's history is most physically present, and the conversations that happen while climbing are the conversations that the Collegium's formal spaces do not permit. Nobody is watching what you say on a rock face. Nobody is taking notes.

The scholarship students and the upper city students climb together in a way that the social geography of the campus does not otherwise produce — shared physical experience, shared mild danger, the specific mutual respect that comes from being in a situation where what matters is whether the person beside you knows what they are doing and not which house their parents are connected to. The bouldering subculture is the most genuinely mixed social space at the Collegium and the one that the administration is least aware of, which is probably not coincidental.

On any other day of the week the streets of the Mid City's central district are what they are — thoroughfares, functional, moving people and goods between destinations. On Saturday morning they become something else entirely, and the transformation is complete enough that a visitor who arrived on Saturday without knowing what day it was would not immediately understand that the market is temporary.

The setup begins before dawn. The vendors who have held their spots for years — the territorial logic of the Hawker Market being older and more firmly established than most of Antitheis's informal institutions — arrive while the streets are still dark to claim their positions and begin assembling their stalls. By the time the light is sufficient to work by, the street is already half-market, and by the time the general population is awake and moving, it is fully transformed.

The canopies go up as the stalls go up, and the canopies are the first thing you see when you turn a corner and encounter the market for the first time. They are not uniform — not a single municipal awning or consistent commercial installation, but the accumulated individual solutions of hundreds of vendors each covering their portion of the street with whatever serves them best. Quilts are the most common covering: domestic quilts repurposed as market shade, their varied patterns and colours overlapping and adjoining across the full width of the street, the patchwork of them creating overhead a visual field that is entirely particular to this institution. Where one vendor's quilt ends and another begins, the light changes. Seen from the street beneath them, the quilts produce a filtered coloured illumination — the specific quality of light that has passed through cloth rather than glass or open air, warm and various and shifting when the wind moves the canopies. The Hawker Market on a sunny Saturday morning, seen from below its quilt ceiling, is one of the most visually distinctive spaces in Antitheis.

Where a passage needs to remain clear for something larger than foot traffic — a cart delivery, a horse, a processional route that cannot be interrupted — the canopies in those sections are rigged to collapse and reassemble. The mechanism is not elegant. It requires the vendors on either side of the passage to release their lines simultaneously, the canopy to fold, the thing to pass, and the canopy to be raised again, all within a sequence that the established vendors have practised enough to conduct without significant disruption to their operations. Watching this happen from above, from a window overlooking the market street, produces the image of the market briefly parting and then closing again like water around an obstacle, the quilt ceiling folding and unfolding as if the street itself is breathing.

### WHAT IS SOLD

Everything. The Hawker Market does not curate. It accommodates.

The food stalls are the most numerous and the most immediately sensory — the cooking happening in real time at the stall face, the specific smells of each vendor's preparation mixing into a combined atmosphere that has no analogue anywhere else in the city. The flatbreads and skewers of the Outer Ring's dawn street food appear here in their Saturday market forms, slightly more elaborate, competing with the Mid City vendors' more varied offerings and the occasional specialist whose product draws a specific clientele from across the city. The person who has been making the same preserved fruit preparation for thirty years, whose stall appears every Saturday in the same spot, whose regular customers arrive early to ensure they get their allocation before it sells — this figure exists in multiple iterations throughout the market.

The second-hand goods occupy the sections furthest from the main thoroughfare, where the foot traffic is lower and the browsing time longer. Everything that the upper city discards eventually reaches the Hawker Market's second-hand section, transformed in its passage through the various intermediary stages of the city's informal economy. The piece of upper city furniture that became a mid-city fixture that became a worn mid-city fixture that became a Hawker Market item has passed through enough hands that its origin is invisible and its price reflects its current condition rather than its history. The person with knowledge and patience finds things here. The person without knowledge also finds things here, some of which are what they appear to be and some of which are not.

The craft goods fill the middle sections — the woodworker's household items, the metalsmith's smaller productions, the textile seller's finished pieces, the leatherworker's belts and pouches and repair services conducted on the spot. The hinge maker from the Outer Ring industrial cluster appears here on Saturdays with the week's production, selling directly rather than through the factor houses that take their margin from the weekday trade. The bladesmith with a stall between the textile seller and the spice merchant, displaying hilts and finished blades alongside the raw materials for those who prefer to assemble their own. The apothecary network's members who use the Saturday market as a secondary distribution point, the specific remedies and preparations available here that do not appear in the registered shop premises and whose provenance is accordingly not discussed.

The scams are present. The market knows this and accounts for it. The vendor with the remarkable find — the Pre-Fall object, the item of impossible quality at an impossible price — is generating a specific response in the experienced market-goer that the inexperienced market-goer has not yet developed. The occasional scam exposed publicly, the vendor caught in a misrepresentation, produces a specific market event — a crowd assembling around the confrontation, the judgment rendered not by any authority but by the assembled opinion of people who have been coming to this market long enough to know what honest dealing looks like. The social enforcement of the market's implicit standards is more effective than any certification system House Ambarris has produced for the purpose.

### THE SOCIAL GEOGRAPHY

The Hawker Market is simultaneously one of the most commercial and one of the most social spaces in Antitheis, and the distinction between these two functions is largely a function of who is attending rather than where in the market they are.

The person who has come specifically to buy something moves through the market with purpose, oriented toward the vendor they know or the category of goods they need. The person who has come to be in the market moves through it differently — browsing, pausing, tasting offered samples, entering conversations that are nominally about a piece of goods and actually about something else. Both populations occupy the same stalls and the same passages simultaneously, their different rhythms producing the specific texture of a busy market where every interaction contains the possibility of being purely commercial or purely social or both.

The upper city visitors who come down to the Saturday market — the occasional resident looking for something specific that their usual supply chains cannot provide, the Collegium students treating it as cultural exploration, the minor house youth who want to be somewhere their position does not follow them — are visible in the market in the way that visitors to any local institution are visible: their navigation is slightly less certain, their response to the prices slightly more revealing, their engagement with the vendors slightly less practised than the regulars who have been coming every Saturday for years. The market accommodates them without particularly adjusting for them. They are customers. They are also, for the vendors who have been watching upper city visitors navigate the market for decades, a source of the specific amusement that comes from watching expertise encounter its own limits.

### THE OUTER RING'S VERSION

The Outer Ring does not have a Hawker Market in the Saturday street-takeover sense. What it has instead is what it has always had — the street stalls that operate daily, the vendor who has been in the same spot every morning for as long as the neighbourhood can remember, the informal commercial infrastructure that the Outer Ring's density makes possible without any weekly organisation. The food stalls of the pre-dawn, the goods vendors who set up when the foot traffic begins and pack away when it ends, the second-hand trade that happens from doorsteps and windowsills rather than formal stalls — this is the Outer Ring's version of the market, distributed through every day rather than concentrated in one.

The Outer Ring residents who come to the Mid City's Saturday market come for the specific things that the daily street trade cannot offer — the craft goods, the specialist preparations, the second-hand finds from further up the city's economy. They come early, when the best items are still available, and they navigate the market with the efficiency of people who have calculated what they can afford to spend and have prioritised accordingly.

Antitheis has been bathing collectively since before the Empire existed, and the institutions that facilitate this have accumulated two centuries of variation, inheritance, and private ownership into a landscape that rewards knowing which establishment you are entering before you enter it.

The oldest bath houses in the Mid City occupy the shells of Pre-Fall octagonal structures whose original function has already been discussed in this account. The conversion from baptistery to bath house required less structural alteration than might be expected — the central basin became the primary soaking pool, the ambulatory became the changing and resting area, and the water infrastructure that had served the original ritual function was redirected toward purely practical supply. What the conversion did not change, because changing it would have meant dismantling works of considerable craft, were the deer spouts.

The deer spouts are cast bronze, fixed to the wall at intervals around the original baptistery's interior, shaped in the form of deer heads with open mouths through which the water emerges in a continuous stream that falls into the soaking pool below. They are Pre-Fall work — the casting quality beyond what the Rebuilding period produced, the patina of genuine age on the bronze — and they produce, when all of them are running simultaneously, a sound that fills the domed space with the specific acoustic quality of moving water in stone. The oldest bath house in the Mid City has eleven deer spouts. The sound when they are all running has been described by a Collegium acoustics researcher as the closest thing in Antitheis to the soundscape of a natural spring, which is something almost no resident of the city has ever heard and which produces in them, without their knowing why, a quality of relaxation that the purpose-built establishments do not quite replicate.

The purpose-built bath houses — newer establishments constructed specifically for bathing rather than inherited from a previous function — have none of this. They have good soaking pools, reliable hot water supply, adequate changing facilities, and the specific character of a business that was designed to do what it does without the architectural inheritance that makes the older establishments something more than functional. They are cleaner, on average, than the baptistery conversions, because they were built with modern drainage requirements in mind and because their floors and walls have not had two centuries to develop the specific porosity that old stone acquires. They are also less interesting, which is a trade-off the clientele makes consciously.

### THE INTERIOR

Entry to the Mid City bath house is through a divided entrance — separate doors or a divided hall leading to separate changing facilities for each gender, the division maintained throughout the establishment by the floor structure rather than by a partition wall. One gender occupies the ground floor, the other the upper floor, and the practical consequences of this arrangement have become socially encoded over time: the upper floor is hotter in summer, which is generally considered less desirable, and warmer in winter, which is generally considered more desirable, producing seasonal reversals in which floor carries the higher status that the regular clientele navigates with the ease of long familiarity.

The changing rooms are the transition space between the street and the water — the benches and hooks where the working day's clothing is removed and stored, the specific social dynamic of a space where everyone is in the process of becoming equal and the markers of status that clothing carries are temporarily suspended. Regular patrons develop specific preferred spots in the changing room with the same territorial logic that governs preferred seating in the ruin gardens. The hierarchy that clothing normally makes visible is temporarily suspended here, though the quality of the towel and the condition of the body beneath the clothing re-establish social information through different channels.

The soaking pools occupy the main interior space — the central pool the largest and most social, the smaller pools around the periphery for those who want less company or more specific water temperature. The water is supplied hot from the heating infrastructure beneath the floor, the temperature maintained by the bath house's stokers whose schedule is calibrated to the establishment's opening hours and the specific demands of a building that needs to be at temperature before the first customers arrive. The steam that rises from the hot pools in the cooler months produces the specific interior atmosphere of the bath house — the partial visibility, the diffused light, the quality of warmth that is inside the body rather than on its surface.

The social function of the bath house happens primarily in the soaking pools and in the resting areas adjacent to them. The information that moves through the Mid City's bath house circuit — the knowledge of who is hiring, what the houses are doing, which business is in difficulty, which marriage is in trouble — is exchanged in the specific register of people conducting private conversations in a public space, which is a register that everyone in the Mid City has developed fluency in. The bath house has no records. Nothing said in the bath house goes anywhere through any official channel. This is the understanding that makes it the city's most reliable information exchange, and it is an understanding that has been tested precisely twice in recorded history and held both times, which is why it continues to hold.

### THE CERTIFICATION SYSTEM

House Ambarris administers the certification of bath houses under the civic health and commerce framework, which produces a tiered quality assessment displayed at the entrance of every licensed establishment and read by the Mid City population with the same fluency they apply to everything else that carries legible status signals.

The full certification requires adequate hot water supply capacity, functional drainage meeting the current standard, clean linen provision, minimum cleaning frequency for all surfaces in contact with water, and quarterly inspection compliance. Establishments with full certification are the most expensive and the most reliably comfortable. Those with partial certification are cheaper and variable. Those without certification technically cannot operate as bath houses, which in the Mid City means they operate as something else with bathing facilities — a distinction the certifying authority pursues with the energy of an institution that has other priorities.

### THE OUTER RING BATH HOUSES

The Outer Ring bath houses are what they are because they have the resources they have, and the resources they have are not adequate for the demand they serve. The buildings are older, the heating infrastructure less reliable, the changing facilities more spartan, the pools smaller relative to the number of people using them. The certification status of Outer Ring establishments is generally partial, which the clientele accepts because the alternative is not bathing, which is the actual alternative for a population without private bathing facilities.

The social function is present in the same form as the Mid City, though the information that moves through it is different in content and the stakes of the conversations are different in character. The democratic pressure of the bath house functions similarly across all versions of the institution. The quality of the hot water does not.

## THE RUIN GARDENS — A VISITED ACCOUNT

The ruin gardens of the Mid City are not maintained in the way that the upper city's private botanical gardens are maintained — not curated, not composed, not expressing a patron's vision of what a garden should be. They are maintained in the way that a useful and beloved public space is maintained, which means the structural concerns are addressed when they become urgent, the vegetation is managed when it threatens the walls, and everything else is left to the accumulation of two centuries of use finding its own equilibrium.

What you find when you enter one is the specific quality of a Pre-Fall stone space that has been absorbed completely into the life of the community around it. The walls are old and the community is old and the relationship between them has had enough time to become entirely natural. Nobody coming to a ruin garden for the first time is struck by the historical significance of the Pre-Fall masonry. They are struck by the shade of the trees that have grown through the collapsed section of the southeast wall, and by the children climbing the intact wall sections, and by the smell of whatever is flowering in the cracks of the stonework this season.

### THE FAMILY GARDENS

The ruin gardens that have developed reputations as family spaces — and the distinction between family gardens and adult gardens is well established in the Mid City's collective knowledge, communicated without signage through the simple mechanism of knowing which one is which — are at their most alive in the late morning and the afternoon.

The children arrive with the specific energy of children released from the Gospel classes' morning session and not yet required anywhere else. The climbing begins immediately. The Pre-Fall walls that survive to their original height provide climbing routes that two centuries of small hands and feet have worn into a specific surface texture — the handholds where the mortar is slightly recessed, the ledges where a boot can find purchase, the sections where the stone face is rough enough to grip and the sections where it has been worn smooth by the accumulated friction of many children discovering the same route. From the upper sections of the climbable walls, on clear evenings, the sunset over the Outer Ring roofscape is visible in a way that it is not from street level — the horizon opened up, the quality of the late light catching the stone and the distant smoke and the campanile tops in the way that certain hours produce in this specific city.

The fallen columns are the garden's primary seating. The columns that collapsed at some point in the centuries between the Fall and the present lie where they fell or close to it, their circular cross-sections worn smooth by the generations of people who have been sitting on them since. They are at exactly the right height for sitting — lower than most constructed seating, which means adults sit with their feet on the ground and children sit with their feet dangling, which is a comfortable arrangement for both. The column drums that have separated from each other make lower individual seats, occupied now by children conducting whatever small negotiations the afternoon's play requires.

The arches that survived the collapse of whatever they once supported have been put to use in a way that their original builders did not intend. Ropes hung from the arch's keystone, knotted at the bottom, produce the most structurally reliable swings in the Mid City — the stone arch far stronger than any timber frame, capable of supporting the weight and the motion without any maintenance concern beyond the rope itself. The gardens with good intact arches at the right height have become known specifically for this feature, the reputation drawing children from neighbouring streets whose own local garden lacks a suitable arch.

The vegetation is part of the garden rather than an intrusion into it. Trees that rooted in the collapsed sections decades ago now provide genuine canopy shade — necessary in the summer heat, pleasant in the milder seasons. The flowers that establish themselves in wall cracks and between the paving stones bloom seasonally without anyone having planted them there, the accumulation of seeds carried by wind and birds over two centuries producing a volunteer garden that the management of the space encourages where it does not threaten structural integrity and removes where it does. The monitoring of structural integrity is the garden's only formal maintenance concern — someone with knowledge of stone and weight and the specific vulnerabilities of Pre-Fall construction walks the walls periodically and notes what requires attention before it requires emergency attention.

### THE EVENING TRANSITION

The children go home when it is time to go home. This is not a precise hour but a social understanding — when the afternoon's light begins to shift toward evening, when the parents in the garden start gathering their things, when the slightly older children who came without parents begin to drift back to their households. The garden empties of its daytime population gradually, over perhaps an hour, and as it empties the evening population begins to arrive.

The Nightbrass groups that play in the ruin gardens after dark do not displace the family use so much as succeed it in the same space. The column seats that served the afternoon's children serve the evening's drinkers. The arches that held the afternoon's swings are now overhead detail in a space lit by lamp and fire. The trees that provided shade now provide the specific quality of tree-filtered lamplight that the ruin garden at night has made its own visual signature.

The adult-oriented gardens — the ones whose reputation is primarily for the evening rather than the afternoon — are simply the gardens where the evening culture is most developed and where the daytime use is correspondingly lighter. These are not less welcoming to children during the day; they simply have fewer of the specific features that make a garden a destination for children rather than just a route through. The distinction between family garden and adult garden is ultimately a distinction between which population has shaped the space most strongly over time, and both shaping forces have been at work for long enough that the results are specific and stable.

### BEFORE DAWN

The Outer Ring does not have a clean transition between night and day. What it has instead is a slow shift in population — the nightlife's last participants moving homeward through streets that the first workers are already entering from the other direction, the two flows passing each other in the pre-dawn dark with the mutual acknowledgment of people who share a city but not a schedule. The person coming home from the Lowest of the Lows and the leather tanner opening their workshop occupy the same narrow passage at the same hour and exchange the specific nod of people who have both chosen, for different reasons, to be awake at this time.

The street food vendors are the dawn's first reliable presence. They appear before the light does, their small fires and griddles lit while the passages are still dark, the smell of hot fat and cooking flatbread preceding any visual evidence of their existence. The flatbreads — herb-flecked, cooked directly on the griddle's surface, sold folded around whatever filling the vendor has prepared that morning — are the Outer Ring's primary breakfast food for everyone who cannot cook at home before work, which is most people. The skewer vendors operate alongside them, the mystery meat a term of genuine rather than ironic accuracy since the source varies with what was available at the previous evening's market and is not generally disclosed and not generally asked about. What matters is that it is hot and protein and costs what it costs, which is the minimum the vendor can charge and still make the morning worthwhile.

The tanneries and the metal workshops open in these same pre-dawn hours because their processes benefit from the cooler air and because their neighbours benefit from having the worst of the smell and the noise concentrated in the hours when fewer people are outside to experience them. The tannery smell is the Outer Ring's most distinctive olfactory signature — the specific combination of the liming process, the animal material at various stages of treatment, and the tannin solutions that give the industry its name — and it carries further than any other smell in the district, which is saying something given everything else the Outer Ring produces. The workers who have spent careers in the tanneries no longer register it. Everyone who passes within three streets does.

The metal workshops — the hinge makers, the buckle formers, the small-part smiths who produce the hardware that the rest of the city's construction requires — cluster in the same sections as the tanneries because both industries have resigned themselves to the areas of the Outer Ring where the smell and noise complaints cannot get worse. The sound of hammers on metal at dawn, multiplied across a full industrial cluster, produces a specific percussion that carries through the stone passages and up through the floors of the buildings above. The people who live nearest to the industrial clusters wake to this sound every working morning and have factored it into their sleep as completely as the city's other rhythms.

### THE HIRING SQUARE

The most significant social institution of the Outer Ring morning is a square — there is one in each of the major Outer Ring districts, and the largest is perhaps forty metres across, not formally designated for its function but used for it so consistently and for so long that its official designation as a civic open space is a legal fiction that nobody maintains with any conviction.

By the time the dawn light reaches the square, the workers who want to be hired that day are already assembled. They arrive early because arriving early is visible, and visible is better than invisible when the foremen and site managers come to make their selections. The square fills from the outside in — the most established workers, the ones with specific skills and recognised reputations, taking positions toward the centre where the hiring agents look first. The new arrivals, the people without established records, the workers who have come from outside Antitheis and have not yet built the relationships that make hiring more reliable, occupy the margins and wait to be considered after the first selections are made.

The selection process is rapid and practiced. The foremen who come to the square know who they are looking for — the glazier whose work on the last upper city commission was noticed, the carpenter whose joints are tight and whose pace is reliable, the general labourer whose endurance is known and whose attitude toward direction is good. Skilled workers are identified and approached directly. General labourers are assessed by the more visible qualities — physical condition, apparent alertness, the specific combination of eagerness and steadiness that experienced foremen have learned to read in a few seconds of observation.

The workers who are not selected by the mid-morning watch the remaining opportunities diminish with the specific expression of people conducting a daily calculation. A day not hired is a day not paid, and a day not paid is a day the arithmetic of the household does not balance. Some of them will try the square in another district. Some will find odd work through neighbourhood connections. Some will not work today and will be in the square again tomorrow.

### THE WORKING MORNING

The children who are not yet old enough for the hiring square are nevertheless working. The Outer Ring does not have surplus — every household member who can contribute does, calibrated to what their age and size make possible. The youngest children run errands, carry small loads, hold positions in queues so that adults can be elsewhere, watch the food stall while a parent restocks. The slightly older children assist in the household's primary work, whether that is helping at the tannery or taking the morning's washed goods to the dry line or moving the previous night's prepared food to the market stall before the day's traffic begins.

The Gospel classes that begin in the mid-morning are the Outer Ring's primary formal education — literacy, basic numeracy, the Gospel of Man's approved history and civic framework, practical skills calibrated to what the district's children are most likely to need. Attendance is nominally voluntary and practically expected, the social pressure to send children to classes coming partly from the institution and partly from the neighbourhood's own understanding that literacy is one of the few individually portable advantages available in the Outer Ring. The children who attend classes do so after their morning household contributions, arriving with the specific quality of people who have already done half a day's work before the teaching begins.

The foot traffic at mid-morning is the Outer Ring at its fullest and loudest. The street vendors at peak operation. The industrial workers in the rhythm of their day. The people moving between the Outer Ring and the Mid City — the cleaning staff, the kitchen workers, the day labourers who secured morning hire for upper city construction work — navigating the narrow passages with the practiced efficiency of people who do this every day and have optimised every step. The sound in the deep passages at this hour is the accumulated output of everything happening simultaneously, which is considerable.

### THE AFTERNOON LULL

The heat of the day reaches the Outer Ring's enclosed passages with a thoroughness that the open districts of the Mid City do not experience. The stone that has been absorbing sun since morning radiates it back from all sides simultaneously, the narrow passages trapping the warmth without the circulation that would make it manageable. The foot traffic thins in the peak heat hours — the street vendors retreat to their awnings, the industrial workers whose processes permit it slow their pace, the people who can sit down sit down.

The children finishing their Gospel classes pour into the streets in the mid-afternoon with the specific energy of people who have been contained and are no longer contained. The Outer Ring's geography, which is a problem for adults trying to move efficiently, is an asset for children — the gaps between buildings that no adult can pass through, the internal courtyards accessible only through specific passages that require knowing where they are, the rooftop routes that connect sections of the district without touching the street level at all. The children navigate this parallel geography with the proprietary ease of people who have mapped it in detail and use it daily, disappearing from visible street level and reappearing three blocks away by routes that the adult population cannot follow.

The games that happen in these spaces are improvised from available material. The roof sledding that we have described elsewhere. The specific Outer Ring variants of games that exist in simpler forms in the Mid City, made more complex by the three-dimensional geography available to play in. The games that involve the sewer entry points, which occupy a spectrum from genuinely exploratory to purely social depending on the participants.

### THE EVENING RETURN

The working day ends at different hours for different trades, but the Outer Ring's evening traffic has a quality distinct from its morning equivalent. The morning flow carries anticipation and anxiety — the hiring square outcome still uncertain, the day's work not yet begun. The evening flow carries exhaustion in the specific form of people who have spent their full physical and cognitive capacity on someone else's work and are now moving the remaining distance toward their own households on what is left.

The bone-tiredness of the Outer Ring evening is not dramatic. It is the quiet, functional, completely unremarkable exhaustion of people for whom this level of depletion is the normal state at the end of a working day. They eat if there is food prepared, and there usually is because the household's domestic labour has been running parallel to the working labour all day. They sleep at hours earlier than the Mid City considers nighttime. The street vendors who set up at dawn are already making their calculations about tomorrow. The children who played in the afternoon gaps between buildings are in the state that children reach when they have fully spent themselves — past resistance, past argument, simply done.

The passages that were full at mid-morning are quiet now except for the nightlife economy that is beginning to stir in the other direction. The same narrow streets, the same worn cobbles, the same buildings pressing close on both sides. Different people. Different purposes. The cycle completing and beginning simultaneously, as it has every day for two centuries, as it will tomorrow.

## SMUGGLING

The Undermarket handles the bulk of Antitheis's illicit trade through its tunnel network, but a parallel surface-level smuggling economy operates at the city's entry points and along the Onnoris that functions differently and serves different markets.

The gate-based smuggling that persists despite House Ambarris's inspection infrastructure exploits three consistent vulnerabilities: the volume of legitimate commercial traffic that makes thorough inspection impossible, the corruption of specific gate officials whose salaries are insufficient motivation for turning down the alternative income, and the specific knowledge of inspection schedules and procedures that accumulates within the commercial community and is shared, for a price, with those who need it.

The goods most commonly moved through surface smuggling rather than the Undermarket are those that benefit from the appearance of legitimacy — items that, once inside the walls with a plausible account of their origin, can be sold through registered premises without attracting attention. Undeclared luxury goods avoiding the Gulf Gate's duty assessment. Commercial quantities of regulated substances travelling under documentation that covers legal quantities. Agricultural products from outside the Empire's tax jurisdiction entering as if they were Interior goods. None of this requires the Undermarket's infrastructure. It requires gate officials who are looking at something else when the relevant wagon passes and documentation writers whose work is careful enough to withstand casual inspection.

The river provides the city's most technically challenging smuggling route and therefore the one with the best margin for those who manage it. The chain systems at the river ports are not absolute barriers — the Onnoris has sections above and below the city where goods can be transferred to smaller vessels that then move through the city's drainage outlets and waterway connections that the official port infrastructure does not monitor. This requires knowledge of the river's specific geography that only the dock workers' community and the Undermarket's tunnel operators fully possess, which creates the city's most consistently profitable information brokerage: selling the specific knowledge of which drainage outlets connect to which sections of the river to people who need to move something without passing a manifest inspection.

House Ambarris knows the smuggling economy exists with a specificity that suggests their inspectors are not simply missing it — they are managing it, which is a different relationship. An economy that is comprehensively suppressed goes underground completely and becomes invisible. An economy that is partially managed remains partially visible, which means partially taxable in informal ways and partially available for use when specific intelligence about specific shipments is needed. The corruption of gate officials is therefore not a failure of House Ambarris's system. It is, from a certain perspective, a feature of it.

## THE MEDICAL DISTRICTS

The medical infrastructure of Antitheis produces, at each of its three levels, a distinct physical environment that the buildings themselves communicate before you know anything about what happens inside them.

The Outer Ring apothecary occupies the kind of premises that accumulates rather than being designed — a ground-floor shop that has acquired its character from decades of the same function, the shelving fitted to the specific containers that the current stock requires, the work surfaces worn in the places where the same preparations are made repeatedly, the smell a specific combination of dried herb, active preparation, and the specific preserving compounds that keep certain materials stable. The apothecary knows the neighbourhood and the neighbourhood knows the apothecary, which produces a relationship of mutual assessment — the practitioner reading the patient's condition against the background of everything they know about that patient's household, the patient reading the practitioner's confidence against the background of whether their preparations have worked before. The folk practice layer is present here more than anywhere else in the formal medical system: the specific herb that the neighbourhood has always used for a specific condition, prepared in a specific way, for reasons that predate the current practitioner and whose efficacy the current practitioner may not be able to explain but has not found cause to dispute.

The parish medical facilities are the loudest and most crowded buildings in the Gospel of Man's Outer Ring infrastructure, and their loudness and crowding is the primary fact about them. The waiting space is designed for a volume of patients that the actual volume consistently exceeds, which means the overflow occupies doorways and the street immediately outside, which means the building's medical function spills into the neighbourhood in ways that the neighbourhood has adapted to — the specific understanding of which times of day are least crowded, which conditions the facility can genuinely address and which will result in a long wait followed by a referral elsewhere, which of the staff are more thorough than others and worth the wait for. The staff themselves are operating at a human limit that the institution does not formally acknowledge and that shows in the specific quality of their attention after six hours of continuous patient contact.

The Mid City hospitals are quieter and smell differently — the specific institutional smell of a building that maintains its cleaning regime, that has the resources to use the preparations that reduce infection, that has physical space between patients and therefore between the sounds and smells they produce. The quality of care here is better not solely because the practitioners are more skilled but because the conditions allow skill to be applied consistently rather than heroically. A practitioner who can focus is more effective than one who cannot. The Mid City hospitals have this capacity. The Outer Ring parish facilities often do not.

The Collegium's medical research spaces occupy the upper floors of the biological science department and have a character entirely distinct from both the clinical facilities below and the theoretical research spaces around them. The smell here is particular: the preservation compounds used for the cadaveric material that the department works with, the specific reagents of biological analysis, the underlying institutional smell of the building itself. The researchers who work here develop a specific professional distance from material that the public, if it thought about the specific processes being conducted, would find difficult to consider abstractly. The department does not advertise its methods. It publishes its results. The gap between the two is managed by the sustained professional culture of people who have decided that the knowledge is worth what is required to obtain it and who have learned not to examine this decision too frequently.

Antitheis marks four significant points in the civic year, and the relationship between official observance and actual practice differs at each one. The Empire has spent two centuries building a festival calendar stripped of divine reference — occasions defined by civic achievement and natural cycle rather than devotional ritual — and the city has spent the same two centuries populating that calendar with whatever human need the official version fails to meet. Both layers are real. Both are visible if you know to look.

### THE WINTER SOLSTICE — HEARTHNIGHT

The longest night of the year is not celebrated so much as survived together, and the distinction matters.

Hearthnight — the name is colloquial rather than official, the Empire's formal designation being the rather drier Civic Festival of Warmth and Community — is the festival that requires the least encouragement and the least official infrastructure because it addresses the most immediate possible human need. In the Outer Ring, where winter kills people with a consistency that House Briganar's census records as a seasonal mortality spike and no one responsible for policy treats as a crisis, the longest night of the year is genuinely the most dangerous, and the response to that danger is the oldest possible response: find the people you love and put them near a fire.

The hearth gatherings of Hearthnight are the kinship-through-shared-hearth principle at its most literal and most necessary. Families pull together. Neighbours who have quarrelled all year find the argument insufficient grounds for leaving someone alone on this particular night. The Outer Ring inns — whose hearths are older than the buildings around them — become communal gathering spaces for those without family, the hearth's social function reverting to its original village-hall form. In the Mid City the ruin gardens are lit with more fires than any other evening of the year, the usual braziers supplemented by temporary fire structures, the atmosphere entirely different from the ruin garden's ordinary nighttime character.

The specific care extended to elders on Hearthnight is one of the festival's most consistently observed elements across all social classes. The generation that survived the Warlording — already diminished, their number reduced to the very oldest residents — is specifically sought out, specifically visited, specifically not left alone. In communities where living memory of the Warlording still exists, however attenuated, Hearthnight is the occasion when that memory is most present and most explicitly honoured. The elderly Snechadan grandparent in the Mid City who remembers what her parents told her receives visitors from the community that she would not otherwise see in a year. The oldest dock worker in the barracks gets the best position by the fire without having to assert seniority.

Beneath the civic festival, the suppressed calendar runs its own parallel observance. The sleeping-figure bread — the loaves shaped in the form of a sleeping person, pressed into the hearth's edge to ensure the household would also sleep safely through the dangerous cold — appears on Hearthnight in a surprising number of Outer Ring homes, made by people who learned the practice from their parents without fully knowing its origin or theological weight. Certain songs circulate on Hearthnight that do not circulate at other times of year, whose words navigate around the cosmological content that the Excision has made dangerous to state directly. The Dreamers find Hearthnight the most emotionally complicated festival in the calendar — the folk practices they have theorised about appearing in front of them, practiced by people who are not making an ideological statement but simply doing what their families have always done.

House Bituris is present at Hearthnight in the way it is present everywhere — monitoring the outdoor gatherings, noting the private ones it can observe, recording the songs it can document. It makes no significant moves on Hearthnight. The political cost of prosecuting sleeping-figure bread on the longest night of the year has been assessed and found too high.

### THE NEW YEAR — FIRSTTHAW

The Empire's new year begins at the first official day of spring, which is determined astronomically and announced by House Briganar's calendar office with slightly more ceremony than the announcement deserves. The actual arrival of spring in Antitheis is a process rather than a day — the cold releasing its grip on the city incrementally, the Onnoris losing the grey-green quality it acquires in deep winter, the first genuinely warm afternoon arriving without warning after a run of merely tolerable ones.

What the city celebrates on Firstthaw is survival. Not achievement, not civic progress, not the Empire's founding principles — simply the fact of having made it through. In the Outer Ring this is not a metaphor. The people who died in the winter are gone and the people who did not are still here, and the first warm days of spring produce a quality of collective relief that the more comfortable districts of the city experience as celebration and the Outer Ring experiences as something closer to the specific emotion of putting down something very heavy.

The markets open with the first spring produce and the quality of the food improves perceptibly overnight — the root vegetables and preserved goods of winter giving way to the first green things, which cost more than they will in full spring but which people pay for anyway because they have been eating preserved food for months and the difference matters beyond its nutritional content. The eating houses update their offerings with a speed that suggests they have been ready for some time, the kitchens having planned for the seasonal transition with an eagerness that the winter's limited palette made inevitable.

The civic address delivered from the Rigosena's eastern portico on Firstthaw morning is the most attended public address of the year, not because it is the most significant but because it is the occasion when the Emperor or his representative confirms, in the formal language of the Empire, that the winter is over and the year's work is beginning. The Outer Ring attends this address in larger numbers than any other civic ceremony — partly because the confirmation of spring's arrival is genuinely welcome, partly because the address is followed by the free distribution of spring food from the civic warehouses, and partly because the occasion of everyone gathering in one place produces the specific social energy that the Outer Ring values and rarely gets to experience in the upper city's direction.

The Onnoris features in Firstthaw in a way it does not in the other festivals. The dock workers have their own river-specific observance — a small ceremony at the waterfront in the early morning before the official civic events begin, conducted by the union's senior figures, that marks the return of the full fishing season and the opening of the Gulf trade routes that winter weather had partially constrained. This is not officially sanctioned. It is also not officially prohibited. It has been happening for long enough that the administration has learned to schedule the civic address to begin after the dock workers have finished.

### UNIFICATION DAY

The anniversary of the first Primarch's founding compact is the Empire's most politically significant festival and, in the Outer Ring, the occasion when politics and pleasure achieve their most comfortable coexistence.

The upper city marks Unification Day with the full weight of its ceremonial infrastructure — the formal procession of the great house representatives through the Inner Ward, the houses displaying their colours from every window and parapet of their compounds in a competition of civic loyalty that is also unmistakably a competition of wealth and taste, the ceremonies at Rigosena and The Open Palm attended by the city's administrative class in their finest dress. The imperial address is the longest public speech of the year, the only occasion on which the Emperor's voice is heard in formal public utterance, and it is attended with the quality of attention that two centuries of the same voice delivering the same annual address has not diminished.

The procession moves through the Mid City and into the Outer Ring, which is the moment when Unification Day becomes a different kind of event from the ceremonies above. The route through the Outer Ring is lined with people who have taken the day off work — which they are entitled to do, Unification Day being one of the few civic holidays that applies regardless of employer — and who have been drinking since a reasonable hour, and who watch the procession pass with a mixture of genuine appreciation and comfortable irreverence that is entirely particular to this community and this occasion.

The Outer Ring knows what the Warlording was. The oldest residents remember what their grandparents told them. The stories of what it meant to live under competing warlords with no stable governance — the arbitrary violence, the extortion, the specific terror of a world where power had no accountability — are part of the Outer Ring's communal memory in a way that the upper city's institutional memory does not quite replicate. On Unification Day this knowledge produces something real: the recognition that what exists, however imperfect and however weighted against them, is not what existed before. This is a low bar and the Outer Ring knows it is a low bar and celebrates it anyway, because a low bar cleared is still a bar cleared, and there is genuine pleasure in having survived to see another Unification Day when the Warlording's survivors did not always survive at all.

The free food distributed from civic stations throughout the Outer Ring on Unification Day is the Empire's most explicit annual acknowledgment of the social contract. It is attended enthusiastically and the food is eaten rather than the sentiment discussed. This is a reasonable exchange.

By evening the Outer Ring is entirely given over to the festival's less civic dimensions — the drinking establishments at capacity, the Pits running at Hearthnight volume, the Lowest of the Lows at its most chaotic of the year. The procession marshals have long since returned to the Mid City. The constabulary on Unification Day evening has learned to maintain visibility without enforcement, which is a calibration that requires experience to achieve and that new constables spend their first few Unification Days learning under supervision.

The Dreamers find Unification Day politically loaded in ways they discuss extensively among themselves and express publicly in ways that are carefully deniable. The storyteller's subtitle for the Unification period — *Under Steady Hands* — circulates on this day more than any other, spoken in the specific tone that makes it simultaneously a statement and a question.

### THE SUMMER SOLSTICE — LONGDAY

Longday is the least formal festival in the civic calendar and the most physically expressive, which follows logically from the temperature.

The tradition of plunging into the Onnoris on the longest day of the year predates any official civic recognition of the occasion and continues regardless of whether the administration chooses to acknowledge it as a festival or simply as what happens every year on the same day. When the heat has been building for weeks, when the Outer Ring's enclosed passages have become genuinely dangerous for the elderly and the very young, when the rooftops are the only place with moving air and even the rooftop air is warm — on that day, when it is the longest and therefore the hottest, the city goes into the river.

The Onnoris swim is not organised. It is not sponsored by any institution or coordinated by any body. It simply happens, thousands of people entering the water from the accessible bank sections between the working port infrastructure, the river briefly becoming the city's primary social space rather than its commercial one. The dock workers — who have a more complicated relationship with the river than most since they work beside it daily — participate in the swim with the specific pleasure of people doing something familiar in a context that makes it new. The sound of thousands of people in water, the specific quality of the Onnoris in high summer when the level drops and the current slows, the way the city sounds different when most of its population is engaged in the same physical activity — these are things you cannot describe accurately until you have experienced them, and most residents of Antitheis have experienced them every summer since childhood.

Those who can afford to leave the city do so around Longday, the surrounding countryside receiving its annual influx of Mid City and upper city residents seeking the specific relief of open air and lower density. The villages within a day's travel of Antitheis have developed accommodation infrastructure specifically for this seasonal migration and price accordingly. The Outer Ring cannot generally afford this option and does not particularly want it, having the river.

The rooftop parties of Longday evening are the rooftop culture at its annual peak — every available roof in the Outer Ring occupied, the Nightbrass bands performing from rooftop to rooftop across the district, the sound carrying in the still summer air in ways that the enclosed winter streets do not permit. The upper city marks Longday privately, the great house gardens and greenhouse terraces serving their intended summer function, the dinner houses serving later than at any other time of year because nobody wants to be inside when the evening is this warm.

The morning after Longday the city reassembles itself with the specific quality of people who have done something together. The Onnoris runs warmer for a day. The Outer Ring streets smell of river water drying on stone.

Antitheis has no single model for how workers and traders organise themselves. What exists instead is a spectrum — from the dock workers' formally structured union to the chimney sweeps' unofficial alliance of mutual self-interest — shaped by the specific power dynamics of each trade and by how much the great houses benefit from organised labour remaining disorganised.

### THE MERCHANTS' GUILD

The most politically significant trade organisation in the city after the dock workers' union is the merchants' guild — a body that does not officially oppose any house's interests and that spends enormous energy opposing specific house decisions that affect its members. The guild coordinates primarily against tax increases and trade regulation changes, which puts it in persistent tension with House Ambarris's licensing architecture and House Briganar's census assessments.

The guild's membership spans a wide range of commercial scale, from the Mid City factor with a single license to the wholesale trading houses with Gulf Coast operations, and managing this internal diversity is the guild's most persistent internal challenge. The large trading houses want protection from the licensing costs that constrain their smaller competitors. The smaller operators want protection from the market dominance of the large houses. The guild represents both positions simultaneously and has developed considerable skill at finding language that addresses both concerns while committing firmly to neither.

House Ambarris watches the merchants' guild with the specific attention of an institution that knows itself to be the guild's primary target and cannot simply suppress it because the guild represents too much of the commercial activity that House Ambarris is responsible for facilitating. The relationship is adversarial, functional, and deeply familiar to both parties.

### THE STEEL ROSES

The Steel Roses are the only formal organisation in Antitheis that exists specifically to go into the places the city has decided not to know about — the Pre-Fall ruins in the surrounding countryside, the deeper passages of the Undermarket's unmapped sections, the forest territories where the wall's protection ends and the world's older inhabitants begin.

They are not soldiers. The Empire has soldiers. The Steel Roses are the organised version of the person who always wanted to know what was at the end of an unexplored passage — hunters, duelists, former guards who found civilian life insufficient, scholars with physical competencies, people whose particular combination of courage and curiosity made them unemployable by any more sensible institution. They take commissions for specific retrieval jobs — Pre-Fall artefacts from identified sites, specimens from creatures the Collegium's biological science department wants to study without going themselves, proof of what is actually in certain sections of the Undermarket that the Undermarket has no interest in disclosing. They also operate on their own account when something seems worth the risk.

The official position of the Steel Roses is that their work is entirely non-supernatural. They find things and retrieve things and occasionally fight things and they do all of this through training and skill and good equipment. This position is maintained with some consistency by the organisation's leadership and with considerably less consistency by the organisation's longer-serving members, some of whom have spent enough time in Pre-Fall spaces, near enough to what survives in those spaces, to have developed perceptual sensitivities that training and skill do not account for. The veteran Steel Rose who notices things others do not notice, who seems to know which passage is safe before checking it, who comes back from extended ruin work with a quality of attention that their colleagues describe as changed — this figure exists in the organisation and is known within it and officially acknowledged by nobody.

Paradigm Fists maintain a monitoring relationship with the Steel Roses that is not quite surveillance and not quite cooperation. The Steel Roses know things about the Pre-Fall landscape around Antitheis that the Paradigm Fists want access to. The Paradigm Fists have authority to shut the Steel Roses down that they prefer not to use because the organisation is more useful operational than suppressed. The Steel Roses know both of these things and conduct themselves accordingly — cooperative enough to maintain the relationship, independent enough to maintain the organisation's actual function.

### THE APOTHECARY NETWORK

The apothecary community of Antitheis has no formal guild and no official structure, and if you ask any apothecary whether such a thing as the network exists they will tell you honestly that it does not. What exists instead is a set of relationships — between practitioners who trained under the same masters, between neighbouring apothecaries who have been passing complicated cases to each other for years, between the herb garden keepers who supply specific materials to specific practitioners and who therefore know which practitioners treat which conditions successfully. Knowledge moves through these relationships. Standards are maintained through reputation rather than certification. A practitioner who consistently makes things worse stops receiving referrals. This is slower and less systematic than formal regulation and more effective than it should be.

The network's relationship to the Collegium's biological science department is cautious on both sides. The Collegium has formal training and current research access. The apothecary network has accumulated practical knowledge, some of which predates the Excision's effects on recorded medicine and some of which works for reasons that the Collegium's framework cannot fully account for. The honest practitioners in both communities know they need each other. The institutional pressures on both sides make acknowledging this need uncomfortable.

### THE CHIMNEY SWEEPS' ALLIANCE

The chimney sweeps have no union, no formal organisation, and no official anything. What they have is a shared knowledge base that nobody outside the sweep community possesses and that they protect through a specific combination of controlled information flow and mutual social obligation.

The alliance — if it can be called that — operates through the apprenticeship relationships and the working partnerships that the sweep routes require. A complex building's flue system requires multiple sweeps who trust each other's knowledge of the connecting passages. This practical requirement produces relationships of mutual dependency that become social relationships, and the social relationships produce the specific gossip and information-sharing culture that makes the sweeps one of the best-informed communities in the city despite their marginal social position.

The information the sweeps share among themselves is primarily practical — which buildings have connected flues that the owners don't know about, which upper city households are having what kinds of difficulties, what is stored in which spaces above the fireplaces of which great house compounds. None of this is formally sold. It accumulates as social currency within the sweep community and occasionally, very carefully, finds its way to people who value it enough to compensate the person who happened to have it, without any formal acknowledgment that a transaction has occurred.

House Catubrix and House Bituris both understand that the sweep community is an information resource. Neither has successfully penetrated it in any organised way. The community is too distributed, too informal, and too defined by working relationships that outsiders cannot replicate. The sweeps have been managing this attention for two centuries and are very good at appearing to know only what they need to know to do their job.

## FAITH FALLEN AND BITTER ENDS

### FAITH FALLEN

Most buildings in Antitheis want something from you — the civic grammar wants your gaze to stay level, the Gospel grammar wants your defiance, Rigosena wants you to look up. Faith Fallen wants you to feel that you have made an error in arriving near it, and it achieves this without quite being able to say how.

The building does not follow either established architectural grammar. It is too tall for civic grammar — thin and aspiring in a way that the earthward tradition rejects — but it does not reach with the Gospel grammar's purposeful aggression either. It reaches wrongly. The buttresses that support its upper sections are too narrow for their span, skeletal rather than solid, producing the visual impression of a building that is straining at its own height rather than commanding it. The towers at its corners are round in the Viriman tradition but stretched, their proportions slightly wrong in a way that takes a moment to identify and then cannot be un-noticed. The iron reinforcements at the wall junctions — functional, necessary given the building's structural ambitions — are not hidden within the masonry as they would be in a better-regarded building. They are exposed, their dark metal visible against the stone, which may be practical and is also unpleasant.

The windows are narrow vertical slits, barely wider than arrow loops, placed at irregular intervals that suggest internal organisation rather than external proportion. The spikes at the parapet turn both outward and inward. The outward-facing spikes require no explanation. The inward-facing ones are not explained officially and are not discussed.

The building is oddly quiet. The Outer Ring generates noise — the district's density produces a constant ambient sound that penetrates most buildings to some degree. Faith Fallen absorbs it. The thick walls and the specific geometry of the interior spaces dampen sound in a way that the building's original architects may or may not have intended, producing a silence within the outer wall that visitors find disorienting rather than peaceful. The silence is interrupted at intervals by sounds that come from no identifiable direction — the specific acoustic channels that the building's geometry creates carrying certain sounds outward through routes that its current operators cannot fully map. You hear what you hear from Faith Fallen and you cannot determine where it is coming from, which is its own distinct quality of unpleasantness.

The building is divided into wards — the ward system reflecting the Outer Ring's gang hierarchy with uncomfortable accuracy, the population self-organising according to the same territorial logic it uses outside the walls, the guards managing this by working with it rather than against it. People enter Faith Fallen for petty crimes and emerge, months or years later, with connections that the district values. This outcome is known. It is not considered a design failure by anyone with the authority to consider it differently.

### THE WALK

On execution days, the announcement comes first.

The crier who reads the charges at Faith Fallen's outer gate has a specific performance style developed over years — formal language delivered at a volume and cadence that the crowd filling the street can follow, with the specific pauses that allow the audience to process each element of the charge before the next is added. The formal language of Imperial justice is dense and procedural, and the crier's job is to make it legible to a crowd that has not read the document. The crowd has learned to listen for specific words — the words that indicate the category of crime, the severity, the identity of the condemned — and to respond to those words with the specific intensity of a community that has clear opinions about what deserves what.

Most condemned walk to Bitter Ends with a guard formation around them — not close enough to prevent all contact with the crowd but close enough to prevent the crowd from making the final determination before the official one. The formation is sized according to the reading of the crowd's energy for each specific case, and the guards who manage the walk have developed considerable expertise in this reading. A political prisoner might draw a crowd that is largely quiet, processing something complicated. A person convicted of crimes against children draws something different, and the formation around them is considerably larger, and the guards are considerably more alert, and even so things get through.

The walk from Faith Fallen's gate to Bitter Ends covers perhaps two hundred metres of Outer Ring street that functions, on execution days, as a different kind of space from its ordinary self. The food sellers set up earlier than usual. The spots with good sightlines on the walk route are claimed in advance by people who want to be part of the event. The economy of the walk route is distinct from the economy of Bitter Ends proper — the walk is free, unstructured, the crowd pressing in as close as the formation allows.

The rotten food and the stones are not supplied by any organisation. They accumulate from the available material of the district, thrown by individuals making individual decisions, the collective result of which is a statement about the community's values that no official document records.

### BITTER ENDS

Bitter Ends is a permanent space — not a temporary assembly but an architectural commitment to the idea that executions are civic events that require civic infrastructure. The stone seating on the three better-facing sides is tiered, worn smooth by generations of occupation, the upper tiers offering better sightlines and costing accordingly. The standing sections behind and to the sides accommodate the population that cannot afford seating and arrives early enough to hold a good position. The space between the seating tiers and the execution platform is managed by the gate workers — not constabulary, not Gospel enforcers, but a specific category of Bitter Ends employee whose entire function is managing crowd flow and access and whose position has passed within certain Outer Ring families for three generations.

The economy of Bitter Ends on execution days is its own complete system. Seat vendors who control blocks of the permanent seating and sell access for specific events. Food and drink sellers whose routes through the crowd have been negotiated with the gate workers and whose products are calibrated for an event that lasts variable amounts of time and produces variable emotional states in the audience. The betting operations — conducted discreetly, through established intermediaries, on the method and duration and the condemned's comportment — that represent one of the Outer Ring's most reliable informal financial instruments.

The crowd at Bitter Ends is not uniform in its response any more than the crowd on the walk route is. The same community that pelts certain condemned with stones on the walk route is capable, for other condemned, of a quality of attention that is nearly respectful — the acknowledgment of someone who did something significant, even something terrible, in a way that commands a different register of response. Political executions draw the most complex crowds. The Dreamers have been known to attend these in numbers large enough to be noticed and small enough to avoid direct action against, which is a calibration they have practised.

The platform itself is stone, permanent, slightly elevated for sightline purposes. The equipment on it changes according to the sentence. The Imperial legal system's range of final sentences is narrower than some other traditions and is officially calibrated to the severity of the crime, which is the official position and does not fully account for what actually determines the sentence in practice. House Bituris's review of judicial appointments has effects that propagate through the sentencing statistics in ways that are visible in the data and not discussed in any official document.

After each execution, the space is cleaned by a specific contracted team and returned to its ordinary state, which is an empty stone amphitheatre in the Outer Ring that children sometimes play in on the days when nothing is scheduled and the gate workers are not present. The children treat it as they treat any available open space — something to run across, to sit in, to use for games that have no relationship to the space's designated function. The adults who watch them from the surrounding streets do not generally intervene. The space is what it is on the days it is that. On other days it is just a stone space in the Outer Ring, and children play in stone spaces.

### FIRST IMPRESSIONS

The Onnoris enters Antitheis through the northern river port and exits through the southern, and along the full length of its passage through the city the waterfront has been worked, shaped, reinforced, extended, and inhabited until very little of the original riverbank remains visible beneath what has been built on top of it. The stone-faced quays that line both banks within the city walls are Rebuilding-era construction on Pre-Fall foundations, the facing periodically repaired and extended as the trade volume demanded. The river itself runs darker here than it does in the countryside north of the city — the combined effect of the drainage and the shadow of the buildings pressing close on both sides, the water moving with the particular quality of water that has been worked rather than left to its own course.

Standing at the quayside for the first time, what strikes most visitors before anything else is the noise. The docks are not quiet at any hour, but at the height of the working day the combined sound of the unloading operations — the calls of the crane operators, the rhythm of the cargo gangs, the specific percussion of goods being shifted across stone and wood, the water against the hull, the occasional cry of something going wrong and the immediate response of people who have learned to respond immediately — creates a wall of sound that the buildings on either side of the waterfront reflect and amplify. The Dockbeat tradition did not emerge despite this noise. It emerged from it, the drum finding the rhythms already present in collective labour and making them deliberate.

The smell changes as you move along the waterfront. This is the most reliable way of knowing where you are on the docks without looking up from the ground at your feet.

### THE ZONES

**The wet market section** occupies the northernmost stretch of the working waterfront, immediately inside the Onnoris North Port. The fishing vessels that work the river above the city — smaller, lighter than the cargo barges, crewed by two or three people — dock here in the early morning hours and unload their catch directly onto the quayside where the market infrastructure is already assembling. The smell here is the strongest on the entire waterfront, the specific combination of fresh catch and river water and the accumulated residue of generations of fish markets that the stone of the quay has absorbed into itself. No amount of cleaning fully removes it. The wet market traders have been working this section for long enough that their stalls and their positions and even their customer relationships have passed from parent to child across multiple generations, and the informal territorial understanding of who works which section of the wet market quay is as precisely mapped as anything House Briganar has in its official records.

The fresh catch moves quickly from here into the city — the Mid City kitchen buyers arriving early, the Outer Ring's daily market runners arriving after, the wholesale buyers for the restaurants and eating houses coming last and taking what remains. By midmorning the wet market is processing the day's second unloading and the first has already dispersed through the city's food supply chain.

**The regular goods and industrial section** begins where the fishing smell gives way to a more varied and less specific combination of rope, timber, grain dust, lamp oil, and the particular smell of stone quay warmed by working bodies. This is the waterfront's highest-volume zone — the cargo barges that bring the Interior's goods to the capital, the agricultural produce of the Heartlands, the processed materials from the northern industries, everything that moves in quantities too large for the road network to handle efficiently. The cranes here are the waterfront's heaviest infrastructure, the fixed-arm lifting equipment installed into the quay itself, operated by teams whose coordination has been refined over careers spent doing nothing else.

The factor houses fronting this section are the largest commercial buildings on the waterfront — three and four storeys of storage and office space, the lower levels warehouse, the upper levels the counting rooms where the trade is actually conducted. The brokers who work these buildings know the cargo manifests of every significant regular barge on the Onnoris by memory, can tell you within reasonable accuracy what will arrive on any given week, and price their services accordingly.

**The luxury goods section** clusters near Tailor's Bridge, and the logic of the clustering is straightforward: the bridge receives cloth from below, the fashion trade that operates on the bridge needs luxury material delivered efficiently, and the merchants handling the Gulf trade's most valuable cargo — the silk, the shellfish dye, the imported exotica that eventually reaches the upper city dinner houses and the great house compounds — want to be as close as possible to the infrastructure that gets their goods to their buyers. The quay here is the most carefully maintained stretch of the waterfront. The buildings facing the water in this section have had more attention paid to their appearance than is strictly necessary for commercial function, because the clients they receive sometimes come down from the upper city and form impressions.

It is also in this section that the waterfront's two steam vessels are moored when not in operation, which is most of the time. They were commissioned by different upper city patrons — one a Collegium engineering faculty member's obsessive decade-long project, the other built by a self-taught mechanic whose patron's primary motivation appears to have been the novelty — and they work, in the specific sense that they move under their own power and can be directed, and they are marvels, and they break down with a frequency that the dock workers' union treats as evidence of a fundamental limitation rather than a technical problem to be solved. The union's official position on steam propulsion is that it is interesting and that its implications for labour organisation require careful consideration before any expansion is contemplated. This position has been maintained consistently for eleven years.

**The second industrial section** mirrors the first on the southern side of the bridge, handling the cargo that comes upriver from the Gulf rather than downriver from the Interior — the salt fish, the basalt worked goods, the raw materials of the maritime trade. The smell here shifts again, the salt and the sea-adjacent cargo giving the southern industrial section a different character from the northern one, the barges larger and more ocean-going in their design, the crews more varied in their origin.

**The destitute housing section** occupies the southernmost stretch before the Onnoris South Port, and it exists because every waterfront has a section where the commercial infrastructure gives way to the people who have nowhere else to go. The buildings here are the worst on the waterfront — older than the neighbouring sections, less maintained, the stone facing crumbling in places and repaired with whatever was available. The people who live here are the waterfront's most marginal population, not dock workers in any union sense but the peripheral community that accumulates wherever there is consistent economic activity and consistent waste from that activity.

The waste disposal infrastructure of the docks concentrates toward this end, which is not an accident. The drainage from the working sections flows southward. The accumulated organic refuse of the wet market and the food stalls and the barracks kitchens travels in the same direction. By the time you reach the housing section the river smell has acquired additional dimensions that the people who live here have long since stopped registering and that visitors find immediately and viscerally apparent.

### THE UNION AND THE BARRACKS

The dock workers of the Onnoris waterfront are the best-organised labour force in Antitheis, and the source of that organisation is the barracks.

The barracks buildings — long, low structures pressed against the inland side of the waterfront road, designed to house the maximum number of workers within walking distance of the quay — create the specific social conditions that produce collective organisation. People who live where they work, who share meals and sleeping quarters and leisure time with the same people they share labour with, who know each other's families and health and financial situations and grievances with the same intimacy that a village community develops, organise differently from workers who disperse to separate households at the end of each shift. The barracks community is effectively a village that happens to be located inside the walls of a city of three million people, with all the social density and mutual obligation that a village produces.

The union that emerged from this community is the most effective in the city. Its leadership is determined by seniority and reputation rather than election in any formal sense — the people who have been on the docks longest and whose judgement has been validated by experience accumulate authority that younger workers extend to them naturally. The union's senior figures are known by name throughout the waterfront and carry their authority visibly — not through any emblem or formal designation but through the specific quality of attention that their presence produces when they move through the working sections.

The union negotiates with House Ambarris on cargo rates and working conditions, with the factor houses on loading schedules, and with the city administration on the waterfront's infrastructure maintenance. It wins these negotiations more often than the other parties would prefer, because the alternative to negotiating is a stopped waterfront, and a stopped waterfront in Antitheis stops the city's supply chain within days. Everyone who matters understands this arithmetic. The union understands that everyone who matters understands it, and conducts itself accordingly.

The union's position on the steam vessels is the most politically significant stance it has taken in recent years. Eleven years of consistent opposition to expansion, framed always as requiring careful consideration, has effectively prevented the wealthy patrons with interests in steam propulsion from scaling their investment. The union is not opposed to the technology. It is opposed to the technology arriving faster than the union can develop a position on what it means for the people currently doing the work the technology would replace. This is a more sophisticated position than most of the city's administrative class gives them credit for, which is exactly the kind of miscalculation the union has learned to rely on.

### THE SENSORY RECORD

A full account of the Antitheis waterfront cannot be given without some attempt to capture what it actually feels like to be there, which is an experience that resists reduction to its component parts but can be approached through them.

The sound is constant and layered — the river itself as a low continuous presence beneath everything, the working noise of the cargo operations above that, the human voice at multiple volumes conducting the thousand simultaneous transactions of a working waterfront, and underneath all of it, at certain hours and from certain sections of the quay, the drum. The Dockbeat in its working context is not performance. It is the sound of people who have found the rhythm already present in what they are doing and made it audible. You hear it before you understand what you are hearing, and then you understand, and it changes the quality of everything else on the waterfront.

The smell moves through zones as described, but within each zone it also moves through time — the early morning wet market at full unload is a different smell from the wet market at midday, the morning industrial section processing fresh cargo a different smell from the afternoon section when the holds are open and the bilge is draining. The waterfront tells you the time of day through your nose if you know how to read it, which the dock workers do and visitors generally do not.

The light on the Onnoris has a specific quality in the early morning and the late afternoon when the angle catches the water moving past the stone quay. The river is dark and rapid at the centre of the channel, slower and more reflective near the banks. The lamp light at night, which we have already described, is the waterfront's most distinctive nocturnal image — the working lanterns producing their specific yellow quality on the water's surface, the barge hulls dark against it, the voices of the night crew carrying across the water with the particular clarity that still air and stone surfaces produce.

I have spent more time on the Antitheis waterfront than I initially intended, which I think is the experience of most people who come to it with any other purpose in mind. It is difficult to leave a place that is working this hard at being itself.

### THE WALLS

The walls of Antitheis rise eighteen metres from their base to the top of the crenellated parapet, and the first thing most approaching travellers say when they see them is that they did not expect them to be so tall. The second thing, once they are closer, is that they did not expect them to look so old.

The walls are old. Parts of them are considerably older than the Empire.

The base course — the lowest four or five metres of the main circuit — is Pre-Fall construction, identifiable by its material and its quality. The dark, fine-grained stone found nowhere else in the region, fitted with a precision that the Rebuilding period's most accomplished masons spent decades attempting to replicate and never quite achieved. No weathering despite its evident age. The mortar joints barely visible. Sections of the Pre-Fall base that have been exposed by later repair work show a surface treatment on the inner face — not decorative exactly, but deliberate, the stone given a texture that suggests original intent rather than pure utility. Whatever the Pre-Fall city's walls were for, they were built to last longer than the city lasted.

Above the Pre-Fall base, the Rebuilding period's additions are immediately visible even to an untrained eye. The limestone is local, warmer in colour, the joints wider, the dressing competent but not exceptional. This is the section of the wall that was built quickly by people who needed it and built it anyway, and the urgency is readable in the stone if you know to look for it. There are places in the Rebuilding section where the courses are not quite level — a few millimetres of accumulated drift over a long run of wall, the kind of error that a more leisurely construction would have corrected and that was simply accepted here because the wall needed to exist more than it needed to be perfect.

The most recent sections — repairs, extensions, the reinforcement of sections that showed structural concern — are the most carefully dressed. The Empire had time by then. The limestone is well-chosen and consistently cut, the quoining at the corners precise, the parapet a proper crenellation rather than the improvised raised sections that appear in some Rebuilding stretches. These sections look newest because they are newest, but they also look most deliberate — the architecture of an institution that has had two centuries to decide what its walls should say about it.

Walking the circuit road inside the wall base is a lesson in reading stone. The Pre-Fall sections look like the wall was always there. The Rebuilding sections look like someone was frightened and building fast. The modern sections look like someone decided this mattered and had the resources to show it. All three are true.

The walls do not merely defend Antitheis. They shape it. A city that cannot expand outward expands upward, and the density of the Outer Ring — the cage homes pressed against each other, the buildings rising floor by floor until the structural load approaches the limit of what the lower floors can bear, the rooftop culture that exists because the roofscape is the only unclaimed horizontal space — is the direct consequence of an eighteen-metre stone circuit that the city cannot build beyond. The walls created the city's vertical character as surely as the ravine did.

### THE TOWERS

At intervals of approximately forty metres along the full wall circuit, round towers project three to four metres beyond the outer face of the wall. The projection is the tactical point — defenders on a tower can direct fire along the wall face in both directions, meaning that anyone attempting to scale the curtain between towers is caught in potential crossfire from the adjacent towers simultaneously. The round form eliminates the blind corners that square towers create and makes the structure more resistant to undermining at the base. The Pre-Fall towers are the most accomplished expressions of this — slightly larger than the Rebuilding-era versions, their curves geometrically precise in a way that the later attempts approach without quite matching.

The tower tops are fighting platforms — open, crenellated, equipped with mountings for the heavier weapons that the garrison rotates between positions as threat assessments change. The interior of each tower contains a spiral stair accessing the platform and the intermediate levels, storage rooms for ammunition and equipment, and the small permanent garrison space where the watch stationed to each tower sleeps and eats. The towers flanking the four main gates are the largest on the circuit, their platforms broad enough for multiple weapon emplacements simultaneously and their walls thickest at the gate approach where the structural and defensive loads are greatest.

The gate towers carry the most elaborate stonework on the whole circuit — carved decorative banding at the platform level, the house emblems of whichever great house funded a significant repair rendered in shallow relief on the outer face, the occasional historical inscription recording a significant decision made at or about the gate. The towers are the wall's face to the world, and the Empire has invested in them accordingly.

### THE FOUR LAND GATES

Each of the four principal gates interrupts the wall circuit at its cardinal point with an arched passage wide enough for loaded cargo wagons to pass without difficulty, flanked on both sides by the largest towers on the local circuit, and surmounted by an inscription in the official script of the Empire that gives each gate its specific identity.

The inscriptions are not uniform. Each gate was built or substantially rebuilt at a different period of the Empire's history, dedicated by a different patron, and the text above each arch reflects the priorities and vocabulary of its period. The oldest inscription — above the Gulf Gate — is in an earlier form of the official script and references a founding Gospeller whose name appears nowhere else in the accessible historical record. The newest — above the Aldare Gate, rebuilt after structural failure in the middle Rebuilding period — is a passage from an Imperial edict on the management of provincial relations, which tells you something about how the Empire's relationship to ceremony had changed in the intervening decades.

**The Brigovan Gate** faces north toward the Northern Territories and the timber roads. This is the gate through which the Interior's working material arrives — the load-bearing wagons of the forestry industry, the supply convoys that provision the northern garrison, the travellers who have come the longest overland distances and who arrive most visibly tired. The gate zone north of the wall is the most sprawling of the four, the approach road lined for several hundred metres with the warehousing and commercial infrastructure that has accumulated to serve the timber trade. Approaching from the north, the gate arch frames the city's roofscape beyond — the Outer Ring's density visible even from the road, the campanile tops just visible above the wall's parapet, the warm smoke haze that sits over Antitheis in still weather rising above everything.

**The Gulf Gate** faces south and handles the highest commercial volume of the four land gates. The Gulf trade routes bring the city's luxury goods, its silk and its shellfish dye and its imported exotica, alongside the more prosaic food supply from the southern agricultural regions. The tax assessment infrastructure at the Gulf Gate is the most developed on the circuit — the weigh-houses and duty stations permanent buildings rather than temporary structures, the House Ambarris clerks stationed here in greater numbers than at any other gate, the commercial licensing scrutiny more thorough because the goods passing through are worth more and the incentive to misrepresent them accordingly greater.

**The Ostavar Gate** faces east toward the Eastern March. The military character of the Ostavar region shows in the gate's design — slightly more fortress-like than the others, the flanking towers broader, the approach passage longer and with additional security positions at intervals. The garrison presence at the Ostavar Gate is heavier than elsewhere because the eastern approach is the one most historically associated with the kind of organised movement that requires organised response.

**The Aldare Gate** faces west toward the plateau. This is the quietest of the four principal gates in terms of raw volume — the plateau people's relationship to fixed infrastructure being what it is, the traffic from the Aldare is more intermittent and less predictable than the other routes. The gate zone west of the wall has a different character from the others, slightly less permanent, more oriented toward the seasonal and the occasional. It is also, for reasons that nobody in the gate administration discusses formally, the gate that House Bituris's monitoring presence is most concentrated at. The things that arrive from the Aldare occasionally require specific kinds of attention.

### THE GATE ZONES

The area immediately inside and outside each land gate has developed into its own distinct commercial and social ecology, shaped by the constant flow of arriving and departing people and the specific opportunities that flow creates.

Outside the walls, along the approach roads, the infrastructure of arrival accumulates. Inns sized for the particular trade of each gate — the Brigovan Gate inns built for timber workers and long-road travellers, the Gulf Gate inns built for merchants expecting a certain quality. Eating establishments calibrated to people who have been on the road and want something hot and immediate. The commercial offices of the factor houses that broker between arriving producers and city buyers, positioned to intercept goods before they pass the wall and complicate the negotiation. The fixers — people whose entire livelihood consists of knowing the documentation system well enough to help others navigate it, for a fee — who establish themselves just outside the gate where their services are needed and just far enough from the gate officers that the relationship is not quite acknowledged.

Inside the walls, the gate zone is louder and more compressed. The money changers who convert regional currencies to Imperial standard. The first establishments to reach arriving goods — the factors who want first access before the wholesale market processes everything into normal commerce. The shops oriented toward people about to leave who have realised they need something. The tour guide culture that attaches itself to visibly disoriented arrivals and offers, for a modest fee, an orientation to the city that ranges from genuinely useful to creative fiction depending on the operator.

The gate zones are where the city's social presentation breaks down most visibly. The upper city's composed self-possession, the Mid City's managed respectability — none of this operates in the gate zone, where the transaction is too immediate and the opportunity too present. New arrivals are assessed within seconds of passing the arch. What they're carrying, how they're dressed, how they're looking around, whether they know where they're going. The gate zone has been reading this for two centuries and is very good at it.

### THE DOCUMENTATION SYSTEM

Entry through any land gate requires documentation, and the specific documentation required depends on who you are and why you are arriving.

Imperial citizens carrying their registration papers — the documents that House Briganar's census system issues at birth, or subsequently at any registered Briganar office — present these at the documentation station and are assessed for their declared purpose of visit or residency. The assessment is more procedural than interrogative for routine arrivals and considerably less routine for anyone whose papers show a flag in the census record.

The registration system's fundamental weakness is the Outer Ring. Briganar attempts to register births at the city's medical facilities, but the Outer Ring's relationship to formal medical infrastructure means that significant numbers of Outer Ring residents were never registered at birth and have spent their lives without documentation. For these people, obtaining papers requires proving citizenship through alternative means — parish records where the Gospel of Man maintains them, community testimony from established registered residents, guild membership for those in organised trades, any institutional record showing consistent presence within the city. The proof requirements are not impossible but they are demanding, and they demand exactly the kinds of institutional connections that the most marginal Outer Ring residents are least likely to have.

Commercial licenses are tiered by the scale of the operation they cover. The smallest license — a single trader, limited goods categories, restricted to specific market spaces — is cheap enough to be accessible to the upper Outer Ring commercial class. Each tier above adds categories, removes restrictions, and increases both the licensing fee and the bond required against duty assessment. The largest commercial licenses — covering the wholesale trade houses, the multi-city operations, the Gulf importers — cost more annually than most Outer Ring residents earn in a decade and require House Ambarris assessment and Briganar registration of every named employee. The tax brackets attached to each tier are the source of a sustained and sophisticated culture of commercial misrepresentation that House Ambarris's inspectors spend significant energy addressing and never fully resolve.

Travellers without either citizen papers or commercial licenses are directed to the registration halls adjacent to each gate — the permanent buildings where temporary visitor papers can be obtained, a process that takes half a day, costs a graduated fee, and produces documentation that specifies the permitted duration of stay, the permitted areas of the city, and the declared purpose of the visit. Temporary visitor papers are renewable. They are also the primary tool through which House Briganar tracks the movement of the non-citizen population through the city, and the registration hall's records feed directly into the census infrastructure.

### THE RIVER PORTS

Where the Onnoris enters and exits the city, the wall circuit does not end — it continues across the water on massive stone piers that support both the defensive structure and the river control infrastructure above. The ballista towers integrated into these river wall sections are the heaviest permanent weapon emplacements in the city, their platforms wider than the standard circuit towers and their mountings fixed rather than portable, oriented to command the full width of the navigable channel.

The river passage is controlled by chain systems — heavy iron chains that can be raised across the channel to stop river traffic when the gate authorities require it. The chains are not raised routinely. They are raised when something on the river requires stopping, which happens rarely enough that the raising of the chain is itself a significant event that the dock community notes and discusses.

The Onnoris North Port and the Onnoris South Port are primarily industrial in character — less of the commercial gate zone ecology, more of the direct infrastructure of cargo movement. The dry docks adjacent to each port provide parking for vessels that need to stop longer than a standard unloading permits, and the warehousing along the port walls is the most directly connected to the wholesale market of any storage in the city. Goods that arrive by river move from boat to warehouse to wholesale factor with minimal time spent in transit, which is the economic efficiency that the river route's volume justifies.

The river port zones have their own documentation requirements, oriented toward vessel registration and cargo manifests rather than personal papers. A barge captain with a registered vessel and a correctly completed manifest passes the port assessment faster than a foot traveller passes the land gate documentation station. The system is calibrated for volume and rewards preparation accordingly.

### A NOTE ON THE WALLS' ORIGINAL PURPOSE

The walls of Antitheis are eighteen metres tall. This is taller than any contemporary military threat to the city requires, and the question of why they were built to this height is one that the official architectural history answers with reference to Imperial ambition and the desire to project power. This is probably partially true.

It is also true that the Pre-Fall sections of the wall — the sections built before the Empire existed — were already substantial when the Rebuilding period began, and that the Rebuilding additions extended and matched a height that had been established by whoever built the original circuit. And it is true, if you ask the oldest members of communities in the regions surrounding Antitheis, that there are stories about things that used to come out of the deeper forests and the less-settled river sections that do not come anymore — not because the forest has changed, but because the settlements grew and the walls went up and whatever used to come eventually stopped coming.

The city has tamed its immediate surroundings. The wolves that remain in the forests north of the Brigovan Gate road are dangerous but ordinary wolves. The bears of the interior hills are large and best avoided but categorically natural animals. The Onnoris carries its standard river fauna and the occasional larger thing that the fishermen have learned to give space. The fantastical and genuinely dangerous has retreated to the edges — to the deep old forest, to the unmapped plateau regions, to the far south where the Gulf meets the open sea.

But the walls are eighteen metres tall, and they were built to that height for reasons that the city has mostly forgotten, and occasionally — in the stories told at the Outer Ring's all-night eating houses, in the accounts that travellers bring from the remoter northern settlements — something reminds Antitheis that the forgetting is a privilege of the protected rather than an accurate description of the world beyond the walls.

### THE DUSK BELL AND THE HINGE

Every evening when the light fails, the bells of Antitheis ring at dusk — the campaniles of the upper city first, then the Mortalis Cathedral towers, then the civic bell-posts of the Mid City and Outer Ring in rough sequence outward. There is no formal requirement attached to this. It is simply dusk, and the bells mark it, as they have marked it for two centuries.

What the bells produce is a transition. When they ring and the household lamps come on across a district — people lighting their homes as the daylight goes, the same act performed simultaneously by thousands of households responding to the same signal — the effect is of the city reassembling itself after the day's dispersal. Streets that were lit by daylight and therefore barely noticed become visible in a new way, the lamp-light carving them into warm patches and specific dark. Something survived another day. Something is still here.

This moment matters more in the Outer Ring than anywhere else. The street lamps there are unreliable — the oil runs out, the maintenance is inconsistent. The household lamps in the windows are often the only light on many streets after dark. The dusk bell is simultaneously a civic marker and the Outer Ring's primary source of public illumination, the moment when the neighbourhood lights itself from within.

Dusk is the hinge between the day city and the night city. What falls on either side of it is substantially different.

### THE OUTER RING AFTER DARK

The deep Outer Ring after dark is not the same city that exists in daylight.

The street lamps here are maintained by whoever can be persuaded to maintain them, at intervals determined by what the district can afford, with oil that runs out at unpredictable points in the night and is not refilled until the supply carts come through in the early morning. The darkness between functional lamps in the narrowest passages is genuine dark — not urban dimness but the particular black of enclosed stone space with no ambient light source. People who live in the deep Outer Ring learn to navigate it by touch and sound and the specific knowledge of where the corners are.

After dark the Outer Ring belongs to its residents in a way it does not during the day, when the administrative infrastructure of the Empire makes its presence felt. At night that infrastructure is mostly absent. The constabulary patrol exists but is thin and knows it. The Undermarket's connections to the surface become more active. The gang territorial arrangements that govern the Outer Ring's informal economy are more visible — not aggressive necessarily, but present in a way the daylight hours conceal.

The danger in the deep Outer Ring after dark is real and specific. It concentrates around intoxication and isolation — the person walking home alone through a lamp-poor passage after too much ale, the visitor who does not know which streets are fine and which are not, the person whose money is visible when it should not be. The Outer Ring's social safety infrastructure after dark is the group. People who live there move together, call out to neighbours, keep to the lit routes. Strangers who do not know to do this find out why they should.

The Pits reach their peak volume after dusk — the Dockbeat louder, the crowds denser, the music felt in the chest where multiple drummers work in low-ceilinged rooms. The all-night eating houses serve the shift workers. The rooftops carry their smaller, quieter, more specific nocturnal population. The Outer Ring at night is dangerous and alive in proportions that depend entirely on where you are in it and whether you know what you are doing.

### THE LOWS

The party district of Antitheis has no official designation. On no administrative map does it appear as anything other than a section of the Outer Ring near the principal ravine bridges. Everyone in the city calls it The Lows, and when people say they want to go somewhere tonight the question is not which pub or which establishment but simply whether they are going to The Lows or staying local.

The Lows occupies the Outer Ring streets immediately adjacent to the bridge approach on the city side — the area where the bridge traffic generates foot volume at all hours, where the flow of people crossing from the Mid City and occasionally from further up creates the consistent human density that commercial night life requires. The establishments here are pressed close together, the streets between them lit well enough to navigate and dark enough to provide the specific quality of privacy that night entertainment requires. Music comes from multiple buildings simultaneously. The smells of food, ale, pipe smoke, and other substances intermingle into something that is specifically the smell of The Lows at night.

The Lows has its regulars — the Outer Ring residents for whom this is the neighbourhood night out, the dock workers finishing a shift, the people who have been coming here since before they were strictly old enough. These are the people who know which establishments are good and which are not, which streets are safe and which require more care, who is worth talking to and who is not. The regulars are the ecology of the place. Everything else happens on top of them.

The visitors from the Mid City cross the bridges in groups, conspicuous in their slightly better clothes and their slightly uncertain navigation of a district whose logic is not quite the Mid City's. They know they are guests here and they behave accordingly — louder than they would at home, trying things they would not try at home, spending money they would not spend at home. The Lows accommodates this happily. The establishments that front the bridge approach are calibrated for this clientele. The ones further in are for people who know where they are going.

The upper city youngsters — the Collegium students, the minor house scions who have come down through the layers of the city specifically to be somewhere their position cannot follow them — are the most visible and most targeted visitors in The Lows. They arrive with money, with the specific excitement of people doing something they have been told not to do, and with the social inexperience of people who have spent their lives in a district where everyone knows the codes. The Lows knows them within thirty seconds of arrival. Their money moves efficiently from their purses into the local economy through a process so practised and so friendly that the victims often do not notice until they are calculating how to get home.

There is a specific figure in The Lows ecosystem — not a gang member, not a formal criminal, just a person of no particular occupation who is very good at identifying upper city visitors, very good at being interesting and welcoming, and very good at ensuring that an evening's conversation results in a significantly lightened wallet. These people are admired locally. They have regulars who are not regulars in any other sense.

The red light streets and the smoke dens occupy the blocks running back from the main entertainment strip toward the darker Outer Ring interior. The sex trade in The Lows is not hidden — everyone knows it is there, including the authorities — but it operates in a register that the main strip's establishments maintain a performative distance from. The smoking dens sit in basement rooms that emit a specific quality of light and a specific smell and are known to their clientele and invisible to everyone else. The other substances — the pharmaceutical products of the Undermarket's alchemist workshops — move through The Lows in smaller quantities and with more care, the transactions conducted in the vocabulary of people who know exactly what they are doing and where to do it.

The crackdowns happen periodically. House Catubrix arrives in force, establishments close, operators disappear temporarily, patrons scatter. The crackdown is thorough and the district is clearly and visibly cleared. Then the crackdown ends, the force withdraws, and within a week The Lows is operating again — sometimes in the same locations, sometimes in slightly different configurations, always in the same district doing the same things with the same people. The infrastructure of the district is not the establishments. It is the bridges, the Outer Ring density, the permanent demand of a city of three million people for somewhere to go at night that is not regulated and quiet and sensible. You can arrest operators. You can close buildings. You cannot arrest the demand. House Catubrix knows this. The crackdowns happen anyway, for reasons that are as much political as practical — they demonstrate that the Empire is not permissive, that someone is noticing and objecting. Then The Lows reopens and the demonstration is over until the next time.

House Bituris sends people into The Lows more quietly and more consistently than House Catubrix's periodic sweeps. The district is where class mixing happens under conditions that lower inhibitions, and lowered inhibitions produce the kind of speech that sober people in separated social environments would never allow themselves. The conversations had in The Lows after midnight include things with religious content. Bituris's people come back from The Lows with information. They occasionally also come back having had a very good time, which creates specific management problems that the house's internal disciplinary processes address on a case-by-case basis.

### THE LOWEST OF THE LOWS

At the far end of The Lows, where the entertainment district meets the ravine bridges and the ground begins to drop toward the gorge, a staircase descends from street level into a space carved into the gorge-side face of the Outer Ring's foundation. The establishment at the bottom of this staircase is the most famous drinking and entertainment venue in Antitheis. It is called The Lowest of the Lows and it earned the name.

The phrase predates the establishment — "let's go to the lowest of the lows" was how people in The Lows described going as deep as possible into the district's entertainments, and when someone opened a venue at the literal lowest accessible point of the ravine approach and named it accordingly, the phrase became a destination. Going to The Lowest of the Lows is now a specific act with a specific reputation. People who have been there mention it.

The space is a combination of construction and cave — the entrance carved from the cliff face, the interior expanding into a natural pocket in the gorge wall that was enlarged at some point in the building's history, the ceiling at the back of the main room being genuine exposed limestone rather than anything built. The acoustics are strange. Music here sounds different from anywhere else in The Lows and the musicians who play regularly know how to use the strangeness rather than fight it.

What happens in The Lowest of the Lows is what happens in The Lows generally, but more so. Louder, stranger, more various, less concerned with maintaining any particular presentation. You are at the lowest of the lows. This is permission of a kind.

The Outer Ring treats The Lowest of the Lows with the specific pride of a community for something locally legendary. It is theirs in a way that the rest of The Lows — accessible to the whole city — is not quite. The visitors are tolerated and exploited with the same practised efficiency as everywhere else in the district. But the establishment itself, the specific quality of the space and the specific culture that has developed in it, belongs to the people who have been coming their whole lives.

### THE MID CITY AFTER DARK

The Mid City at night is the most comfortable version of itself.

Most residents spend their evenings in the neighbourhood — the local establishment on the corner, the ruin garden that has not closed yet, the bath house that stays open late. The Mid City has enough density of good neighbourhood places that the evening does not require going anywhere ambitious. People stay local, stay comfortable, and go home at a reasonable hour.

The younger section makes different choices. The ruin garden is fine but they have been to the ruin garden. The local pub is fine but the same people are always there. The Lows across the bridge are louder and stranger and more dangerous and require crossing into a district that is not quite theirs — and that combination of risk and novelty is precisely what draws them. On a busy night in The Lows you will find a significant representation of Mid City youth who crossed the bridge specifically because they wanted to be somewhere their parents would not look for them.

The Mid City streets after dark are properly lit and reasonably safe. The ruin gardens close when their operators decide they are closing. The Catuvar games in the bath houses run until the players run out of money or patience. The gambling den in the Pre-Fall basement operates at its fullest capacity between dusk and the early morning, the most serious matches played by lamplight in a room that maintains its quality of being outside the normal city's logic regardless of what time it is above.

### THE RAVINE AFTER DARK

The gorge floor never fully stops but changes character with the light.

At dusk the dock lamps come on along the stone-faced banks — the practical yellow of working lanterns, not the warm domestic light of household windows. The river reflects this back from below in an uncertain way that makes the gorge floor feel simultaneously more and less solid than it does in daylight. The sound of water becomes more present at night when the other sounds diminish. The echoing quality of the gorge walls produces, in the quietest hours, a near-continuous murmur of reflected water noise that the barracks workers sleep through and visitors find difficult to ignore.

The fashion bridge after dark is its own thing. The workshops are closed but the bridge remains inhabited, and the bridge's culture of self-presentation does not fully switch off at night. The lamp-lit inhabited bridge over the dark gorge is one of the images of Antitheis that visitors describe in their letters home — the warm light of the span, the water invisible below, the cliff-face terraces dropping away into relative dark on either side.

### THE UPPER CITY AFTER DARK

The upper city at night is consistently, expensively lit. Every street lamp is maintained and every street lamp is lit. The effect is of a city that has decided night is not a special condition but simply another time when things are conducted accordingly.

The formal dinner houses serve until late. The house compounds host their private gatherings — music occasionally audible from behind compound walls, reaching the empty street outside as something heard rather than listened to. The greenhouse lights glow warm through glass panels against the cold stone. The campanile tops are occupied by students writing in the specific privacy that height and wind provide.

The upper city's contribution to The Lows is its young people — the ones who have heard about it and come down through the layers of the city in groups, dressed almost correctly, carrying slightly too much money and slightly too much visible excitement. They go back up before dawn, usually. They have stories. They occasionally do not have wallets. The upper city discusses their evenings in The Lows the next morning with the specific mixture of disapproval and curiosity that has always characterised the comfortable classes' relationship to the entertainments of those below them.

### THE NIGHT WORKERS

The **night soil collectors** begin their rounds after dusk, moving through the Outer Ring and Mid City with their distinctive carts — heard before seen, known to every Antitheis resident as the sound of a certain hour.

The **pre-dawn bakers** begin heating ovens in the middle of the night. The smell of baking bread before dawn — the warmest smell in a city that smells of smoke and stone and human density — is one of the consistent pleasures of early rising in Antitheis.

The **chimney sweeps** depart for the upper city in the deep pre-dawn, gathering at known assembly points before the walk up through the still-dark city. Their knowledge of the upper city's interior is accumulated in these pre-dawn hours, in silence and darkness.

The **late barge workers** operate to the river's schedule. The gorge floor's lamp-lit docking areas are their world — a night shift the city above barely registers.

### THE QUALITY OF DARKNESS

In the upper city, night is a quality of light. The street lamps produce warm circles, the spaces between them navigable shadow. Night here is aesthetic.

In the Mid City, night is a patchwork. The lit arcades and ruin gardens alternate with the darker back lanes. Night here is manageable.

In the Outer Ring, night is a condition. The lamp-poor deep passages after the oil runs out are genuinely dark in a way that changes behaviour. The people who live there know which corners are corners and which are walls. Night here is information.

In The Lows, night is the point. The whole district exists because of what darkness permits, and the quality of light there — enough to see, not enough to be seen too clearly — is precisely calibrated to that function.

The Undermarket, below all of it, operates in permanent dark that has nothing to do with the time of day above. Down there the difference between night and day is purely academic.

## THE DIASPORA DISTRICTS

Antitheis is the largest city on Ath and the economic centre of the Empire, which means it is the destination of anyone who has run out of other options, anyone whose trade requires the largest possible market, and anyone whose home became uninhabitable for reasons political, environmental, or personal. The city absorbs these arrivals and distributes them according to the logic it applies to everything else — by what they have, by what they can do, and by how much they visually and culturally resemble the population already there.

The result is a city that is more diverse than its official self-presentation acknowledges and less integrated than its diversity would suggest. The diaspora communities of Antitheis live in the city without quite living in the same city as each other or as the native Antitheis population. They share streets and markets and occasionally bath houses. The social worlds behind those shared surfaces are largely separate.

### THE HUMAN DIASPORA

The largest foreign communities in Antitheis are human, which means they are foreign in culture, dress, and language while being visually comprehensible to the native population. This comprehensibility allows for a degree of integration that the non-human communities cannot access, but it does not eliminate the friction of difference — it simply makes that friction more negotiable.

**The Snechadans** are the most visible human diaspora community, primarily because the Nordic character of their physical appearance — the specific colouring, the height, the particular quality of their dress — makes them immediately identifiable in a city whose population runs toward the darker, more compact Viriman type. They cluster in the Mid City's northern sections, where the industrial and maritime trades that many of them came to practice are most accessible. The Snechadan community is established enough to have its own eating houses, its own informal meeting spaces, and its own internal social hierarchy that has almost nothing to do with how the wider city views them. They are considered hardworking and insular in roughly equal measure by the Antitheis population, both of which are reasonably accurate as observations and neither of which captures the community's actual character.

Their relationship to the Excision is complicated. Snechada has its own religious traditions — not the same as the old Ath practices, but devotional in ways that House Bituris considers sufficiently analogous to monitor. The Snechadan community practices its traditions quietly and in private and has developed over generations a fairly sophisticated read of exactly how much visibility is safe. The older members of the community are experts in this calibration. The younger ones, who have grown up in Antitheis and feel more entitled to the city than their grandparents did, sometimes push the calibration in ways that make the older generation nervous.

**The Elike community** is more dispersed and more cosmopolitan than the Snechadans, reflecting Elike's own internationally oriented character. They are not concentrated in a single district so much as distributed throughout the Mid City and upper Outer Ring in a pattern that reflects their varied occupations — factors, translators, scholars, traders in the specific goods that move between Elike and Ath. The Elike presence in Antitheis has been there long enough that distinguishing the diaspora community from the general Mid City population requires more than casual observation. Some of the Collegium's faculty are Elike-descended, their families having been in Antitheis long enough that the distinction has become academic.

The smaller human communities — individual traders from Bazka, occasional travellers from other continents who stayed — are present in ones and twos throughout the Outer Ring and the docks district, too sparse to constitute a community in the organised sense but collectively contributing to the specific quality of the docks and the Lows as places where the expected rules of Antitheis social composition don't quite apply.

### THE NON-HUMAN COMMUNITIES

What exists in Antitheis from the non-human populations of other continents is not a community so much as a residue — the people who arrived through the docks and did not or could not go home, concentrated in the areas of the city where the Excision's enforcement is lightest and the questions asked are fewest.

The docks and the adjacent Outer Ring sections are where this population is most present. The specific logic is economic: the docks create demand for labour that does not require documentation, does not require the specific social presentation that the Mid City assumes, and does not ask questions about origin. The Undermarket creates similar demand for physical capability and specific knowledge that the surface city does not bother to cultivate. Between the docks and the underground economy, a small population of people from Bazka, Kala, and Su-Lurrak finds something approaching a livelihood.

**The Bazka arrivals** — the plant-based, mushroom, and animal-aspect people of that continent — are present in the smallest numbers and are the most visually startling to an Antitheis population that has limited frame of reference for humanoid beings who consume the world differently or carry their biology differently. They are concentrated near the docks and the lower Outer Ring, rarely seen further into the city, and are known to most Antitheis residents primarily by rumour rather than direct experience. The Undermarket has use for people whose physical and biological distinctiveness the surface city finds uncomfortable — a specific kind of labour that requires no credentials and asks only that you show up and that nobody asks too precisely what you are.

The red light dimension of their presence in the city is real and is not the same thing as the rest of their existence, though the wider city tends to conflate the two. The Outer Ring has a specific demand for exotic encounters that the Lows and its adjacent streets partially service, and people who are visually and biologically distinct from the Antitheis norm find themselves positioned as commodities in that economy whether they intended to or not. This is a condition the older members of these micro-communities navigate with the weariness of people who have been navigating it for years. It is not their whole existence. It is a dimension of existing in a city that has decided what they are useful for.

**The Kala Sirens** — the land-capable branch of Kala's siren-adjacent races — are the rarest non-human presence in Antitheis and the one that produces the most significant social reaction when encountered. A Siren in the Outer Ring or the Lows stops conversation in a way that even the Bazka arrivals do not, partly because the Kala races carry associations with the sea and with the divine that the Excision's suppression has not fully eliminated from the cultural imagination. House Bituris has a specific interest in them that is not entirely explained by the standard monitoring rationale. The Siren community, such as it is, has learned to be extremely careful about what it is seen doing and who it is seen talking to.

**The Su-Lurrak presence** is small and mostly passing through — traders who come with specific goods and leave when the trade is done. The ones who stay do so for reasons that are not always clear to the community around them. They occupy the same docks-adjacent territory as the other non-human arrivals and are treated with the same mixture of curiosity and wariness.

### THE UNDERMARKET CONNECTION

The connection between the diaspora communities and the Undermarket is partly economic and partly protective. The non-human populations have limited access to the surface city's registered economy — House Briganar's documentation system was not designed with them in mind and does not function well for people who do not fit its categories. The Undermarket, which has no documentation system and does not ask about categories, offers a route to economic participation that the surface city closes.

This creates a dependency that the Undermarket exploits. The gang organisations that run the underground economy are not benevolent. Their interest in the non-human communities is instrumental — useful people are valuable, useless people are not, and the definition of useful is determined by the organisation rather than the individual. The physical capability of some Bazka and Kala arrivals makes them attractive to the Undermarket's enforcement functions. Their social isolation makes them difficult to advocate for when things go wrong.

The diaspora communities are aware of this dynamic. The more established human communities — the Snechadans, the Elike traders — have enough surface city integration to maintain some distance from the Undermarket even when they use its services. The non-human arrivals, with less access to that integration, are closer to the underground economy and more exposed to its consequences.

### HOUSE BITURIS AND THE FOREIGN PRESENCE

House Bituris monitors the diaspora communities with a specific interest that goes beyond the standard Excision enforcement rationale. The foreign communities, particularly the non-human ones, carry religious and devotional traditions from continents where the Excision does not operate. The Su-Lurrak relationship to divinity, whatever it is, exists outside the Imperial framework entirely. The Kala traditions around the sea and its inhabitants carry associations with oceanic entities that the old Phoenician-rooted Gulf culture also references.

The Dreamers have begun to notice this. The intellectual counter-culture's interest in what the Excision erased from Ath is beginning to extend to what arrived in Antitheis from places the Excision never reached. A Collegium student who has read the Department of Immaterial Science's papers on the belief-to-reality mechanic, who then sits down with a Snechadan elder who still practices the devotional traditions of their home continent, is connecting two things that Bituris would prefer remained unconnected.

The diaspora communities did not come to Antitheis to be useful to the Dreamers. The Dreamers' interest in them is not always distinguishable, from the outside, from the wider city's tendency to treat them as resources rather than people. The more politically aware members of the Dreamers are conscious of this. It does not always change the behaviour.

## THE MORTALIS CATHEDRAL — A VISITED ACCOUNT

### THE APPROACH

The spires are visible before almost anything else about the cathedral is visible, and this is the point. They rise from the surrounding upper city stonework like blades — not the rounded towers of the civic tradition but something sharp and intentional, their profiles cutting the sky in the way that something designed to cut cuts. From the Mid City, from the ravine bridges, from certain Outer Ring rooftops on clear days, the Mortalis Cathedral's spires read against the sky as an argument. They are the Gospel grammar at its most unambiguous: we are coming for what is up there.

The exterior is dark grey-green stone — not the warm limestone of the civic buildings or the pale Pre-Fall material of the oldest structures, but a dark, cold material whose colour changes with the light and which in overcast weather seems to absorb what illumination exists rather than reflect it. The building does not glow. It looms. The fist rose window on the west face — through which you enter — is the building's concession to decorative intent, the lead and stone tracery forming the closed knuckles of the fist through which the light enters the nave as what light entering through a fist enters as: filtered, broken, arriving at angles that prevent it from illuminating anything cleanly.

### ENTERING

You enter under the organ.

The west wall of the nave is the organ's rear face — the instrument's pipes and casework filling the full width of the space from floor to the point where the vault springs, the entrance passage passing through the lower section of the organ case beneath the pipes. The hands begin here. The pipe case is carved with stone hands emerging between the pipes — hands of various sizes, some gripping the pipes themselves, some reaching outward from the casework into the nave, the carved stone fingers and palms interrupting the regular geometry of the pipe array. You pass between them entering the building and you pass between them leaving it. The organ's case is not a frame for the instrument. It is the instrument's body, and the hands are part of its body.

The sound of the organ when played fills the nave with a physical presence that the dark stone amplifies and holds rather than dispersing. The vaulting above — normal Gothic ribbed vaulting, the ribs rising from clustered columns to meet at the vault's apex in the standard configuration of the tradition — collects and returns the bass registers in a way that the body registers before the ear does. During services the organ is felt in the sternum and the throat and the spaces behind the eyes. The cathedral's acoustic is not an accident. It was designed for the specific physical effect of divine awe produced without the divine.

### THE NAVE AND THE CLOCK

Moving east down the nave, the space does what Gothic space always does — it directs you forward, the axis established by the pews on both sides, the floor of worn dark stone flags beneath which the crypts begin. The dead are below you from the moment you enter. The floor is not marked to indicate this. You learn it or you do not.

The astronomical clock occupies the north transept arm, its face filling the wall from the floor to the height where the transept vault springs. It is the largest object in the cathedral after the organ and the carved arm, and it is the most explicitly civic element in a building that is otherwise straining against its own civic classification. The clock face is a system of concentric rings — the outermost marking the Imperial calendar in the official script, the inner rings showing seasonal progressions, administrative periods, the cycles of the solar and lunar year as the Empire has chosen to enumerate them. The carved decoration surrounding the clock face is the building's most restrained figurative work: small carved figures at the corners representing the four seasons, rendered as human figures in seasonal dress rather than as allegorical or religious symbols. The clock strikes the hours with a mechanism that the building's acoustic makes audible throughout the nave as a presence rather than a sound — a vibration in the stone that precedes the audible strike and follows it.

The Collegium's engineering faculty considers the clock one of the Horologist's indirect effects — precision mechanical work of a quality and sophistication that suggests influence from something that cares about the accurate measurement of time in a way that goes beyond the administrative. The faculty publishes this opinion carefully and without drawing the implication that the implication suggests.

### THE ARM

At the crossing — where the nave meets the transept — the vault above is higher than anywhere else in the building, the full height of the cathedral gathered at this single point. And from that point, from the apex of the crossing vault where the ribs converge, a single arm descends.

It is carved from a stone darker than anything else in the building — a black so deep it reads as an absence in the dark grey-green of the surrounding material, as if the arm were not carved into the cathedral but had come through it from somewhere above. The carving is of exceptional quality: the anatomy of the arm is accurate to a standard that the Collegium's biological science faculty has noted in their internal documentation as inconsistent with any known Rebuilding-period workshop. The hand at its end is open, palm down, fingers extended downward in the gesture of reaching rather than grasping. It does not grip. It descends.

Along the arm's full length, spiralling downward from the shoulder toward the hand, smaller hands are carved in relief — dozens of them, progressively smaller, each one reaching in the same direction as the one above, the whole assembly creating the impression of a single gesture repeated at every scale simultaneously. The smallest hands near the base of the arm are barely larger than a child's. The largest, near the point where the arm meets the vault, are wider than a man's shoulders. They all point down.

The arm descends to approximately four metres above the floor — above the position where the preacher stands at the pulpit during services, so that the person delivering the Gospel of Man's civic message stands directly beneath the open hand and does not look up at it while speaking. The congregation, seated in the pews, looks directly at both simultaneously: the human figure speaking below, the stone hand reaching toward the human figure from above. The relationship between them is never stated. It is simply visible.

Two centuries of the city's population have sat in these pews and looked at this arm. The belief energy that has accumulated in the carved stone over those two centuries is real in the sense that anything in this world that is sufficiently believed in becomes real — not the stone itself, which is stone, but what the stone has been made to hold through sustained collective attention. Paradigm Fists who enter the building on institutional business report nothing and feel something. The Dreamers who attend services specifically to sit beneath the arm come away with notes they do not share widely. Krad Kraps visited the cathedral four times in his time in Antitheis and spent longer beneath the arm on each visit than the visit's stated purpose required. He has not written about what this was like. He has written around it, which is the same thing acknowledged differently.

### THE EAST END

Behind the preacher's position, filling the entire apse wall, is the carved altarpiece — the building's most complex figurative work and the one that requires the most knowledge to read.

The programme is historical rather than theological: the founding of the Empire rendered in carved stone relief panels, arranged in tiers from the floor to the vault. The bottom tier shows the Warlording in the style of a battle chronicle — figures in combat, settlements burning, the specific chaos of ungoverned violence rendered with a detail that suggests the carvers worked from accounts they took seriously. The middle tier shows the Unification: the first Primarch extending the open hand to the assembled warlords, the founding compact, the establishment of the great houses in their original form including House Carnaris in its position before its destruction, its carved figures still present in the altarpiece because removing them would require explaining why they were removed. The upper tier shows the Silver Era in progress — the building of Antitheis, the establishment of the Gospel infrastructure, the expansion of the Empire into its current form.

The figures at every tier are human. No divine beings appear anywhere in the altarpiece. The programme is strictly the history of human governance and human achievement, rendered with all the technical resources of devotional carving applied to secular content. The result is a carved history that feels like a devotional object because it was made with devotional skill, and that means something that the institution has not formally acknowledged.

### THE CRYPTS

Below the floor, accessed by stairs at the transept crossing, are the cathedral crypts — the burial space for the Empire's most significant historical figures, the founding administrators, the senior Gospellers whose institutional contributions warranted permanent presence beneath the building's most important floor. The crypts are lit by oil lamps maintained by the cathedral's permanent staff, and the quality of light down there — low, amber, moving slightly with the air that comes through the stairwell — is entirely different from anything above ground.

The crypts are open to the public during cathedral hours. They are not heavily visited. Most people who enter the cathedral come for the nave, for the arm, for the clock, for the organ on feast days. The crypts are for people who specifically want to be in the place where the city's dead are most formally kept, which is a specific kind of person and not a numerous one. They tend to stay for longer than the space strictly requires.

The floor above them, when the organ plays during services, transmits the bass registers downward into the stone. In the crypts, during a service, the music arrives as a vibration in the walls and the floor rather than as sound. People who have experienced this describe it in terms that they subsequently qualify and reconsider. The qualification never quite succeeds in explaining away what they originally said.

### THE PROPORTIONS

The Mortalis Cathedral's pews seat perhaps eight hundred people. The nave could hold three times that standing. The transept arms extend further than any functional requirement for the services conducted in them justifies. The crypts below are larger than the number of historically significant burials requires. Every dimension of the building is calibrated for a population larger than the one that currently uses it — as if the building was designed for a congregation that has not yet arrived, or for one that used to come and no longer does, or for one that is not composed of people at all.

This quality is not unique to the Mortalis Cathedral. Dark Souls spaces have it — spaces built to the scale of something that believed in them before it left, leaving the space behind still oriented toward that belief. The cathedral waits in this way. Fully present, slightly empty, the proportions correct for something that isn't currently standing in them. On an ordinary day, with a modest weekday attendance scattered through the vast pews, the building's scale makes the congregation look like the remnant of something rather than the thing itself. On the high feast days, when the building is as full as it ever gets, it is still not full. There is still more space than people. There is always more space than people.

What the building was built expecting — what scale of presence it was proportioned to receive — is not recorded in any document that has survived. The carvers who worked the arm knew something. The architect who specified these dimensions knew something. The institution that commissioned it may have known or may not have. The Emperor, who approved the plans and has maintained the building for two centuries without altering its proportions, knows.

The five great house compounds of the upper city cluster around the Throne in a rough semicircle whose geography reflects two centuries of political positioning made architectural. They are all manor houses in their basic typology — residential and administrative buildings arranged around enclosed grounds — and they are otherwise entirely distinct from each other, each one expressing the character of the institution it houses through every choice that went into its construction.

### HOUSE CATUBRIX

The Catubrix compound announces its character before you reach its gate. The towers are visible from the approach street two hundred metres away — round, thick-walled, rising a full storey above the compound's main building, their parapets crenellated and their surfaces undecorated except for the mounting positions for the scorpions that face outward from three of the four cardinal points. The scorpions are not display pieces. They are maintained, loaded, and manned on a rotation that the garrison treats as regular duty rather than ceremonial function. In a city that has not faced external military threat within living memory, the Catubrix compound is the only private structure that behaves as though it might.

The gate is the compound's primary architectural statement — the largest private gate in the upper city, flanked by the largest tower sections, its arch wide enough for a laden supply wagon and reinforced with ironwork that is functional rather than decorative. The approach is an open forecourt, which means arriving at the Catubrix compound requires crossing an exposed space with no cover while the gate's defenders have full visibility of your approach. This is not accidental. The space between the street and the gate is the distance within which a visitor can be assessed, identified, and either admitted or turned away while having no structural position to defend.

Inside the gate, the courtyard is stone-flagged and largely open — no ornamental garden, no attempt at softened atmosphere. The interior of House Catubrix looks like what it is: a fortress that happens to be inside a city.

### HOUSE BRIGANAR

The Briganar compound's most distinctive feature is its light, which is strange to say about a stone building in a city of stone buildings but is nonetheless the first thing every visitor notices and remembers.

The building is tall — taller than its footprint requires, the rooms inside rising two full storeys in the main archive and reading spaces with a third partial storey above. The walls at the upper levels are interrupted by clerestory windows — continuous horizontal bands of glazing set high in the walls — and in the central archive hall the roof itself is partially glazed, the glass panels set into the stone vaulting in sections that track the sun's path. In the morning the eastern windows flood the reading tables. By midday the roof panels carry light from above. In the afternoon the western clerestory catches the golden hour. The archive hall provides good working light at every hour of the working day.

The tall rooms this produces create a quality of vertical space that is unusual in the Viriman civic tradition — not the aspiring vertical of the Gospel grammar but the purposeful height of spaces that needed to be tall for practical reasons and have been made beautiful in the process. The shelving reaches the full height of the walls, the accumulated documents of two centuries filling every shelf. The smell is particular: old paper, leather binding, the specific dryness of preserved documents in a stone room.

The compound's exterior is modest — the building's real investment is entirely interior, the outside face presenting to the street with plain limestone. You would not know, from outside, what is inside.

### HOUSE YAMBAL

The Yambal compound turns its back on the street.

The exterior face is almost entirely blank — limestone walls of standard height, a single entrance door with no forecourt and no architectural elaboration, two narrow windows set too high to illuminate anything at street level. It reads as a building that would prefer not to be looked at.

The interior is what the exterior denies. The entrance passage turns immediately, preventing any view of the interior from the street, and opens into a courtyard that has no relationship to anything visible from outside. The central pool is fed by a channel producing a continuous sound of moving water. The tiles on the courtyard walls are the compound's most significant decorative investment — geometric patterns in the Gulf tradition, deep blues and greens and the specific turquoise that the Gulf coast's ceramic workshops produce, in compositions referencing the mathematical traditions of the trade routes the house operates on. The pattern is not Viriman. It has never been Viriman. The Empire has never formally objected to this, because Yambal's maritime revenue is not something the Empire can afford to make a point about.

The residential and administrative buildings face inward onto the pool and the garden rather than outward onto the street. The building gives everything to its interior and nothing to the city around it, which is both a cultural statement and a security choice.

### HOUSE BITURIS

There is something wrong with the Bituris compound and it takes time to identify what it is.

The approach is through a passage between two compound walls — narrower than a standard street, the walls rising higher than the adjacent buildings, eliminating any lateral reference point. The passage is not dark exactly but the light is indirect and directionless, the shadows falling in a way that makes the wall surfaces difficult to read. By the time you reach the entrance gate you have been enclosed long enough to lose your casual sense of spatial orientation.

The gate opens into a courtyard smaller than the compound's external footprint suggests it should be. The building faces inward — away from the entrance, away from the street, away from the visitor. Its primary face is the back of itself. The windows on the entrance-facing walls are few and narrow, set at heights that would illuminate corridors rather than rooms.

The stonework is in excellent repair. Too excellent — the maintenance has been regular enough that the stone shows patches of different weathering ages across its surface, meaning the building has been regularly modified in ways that have left a record in the stone for anyone who knows how to read it. The internal layout has not been constant.

The compound is quiet in a way that buildings with people in them are not usually quiet. Not empty — occupied, functioning — but quiet in the way that a place whose occupants have specifically decided to produce minimal external evidence of their activities is quiet.

House Bituris has a wing that does not appear on any submitted architectural plan. This is known in the way that things about House Bituris are known — as something concluded from evidence rather than told, not discussed in contexts where discussion would be recorded.

### HOUSE AMBARRIS

The Ambarris compound is the newest significant construction in the upper city and this is visible in ways that the house's architects worked to conceal and did not fully succeed in concealing.

The stone is lighter in colour than the surrounding buildings — it has not had time to weather to the grey that everything else in the upper city has acquired over centuries. The compound is slightly too large for its administrative function, the forecourt slightly too spacious, the entrance arch slightly wider than it needs to be. Every dimension is calibrated to what the house concluded a great house compound should be after studying the other four, and the calculation is visible in the result in the same way that a very good reproduction is visible as a reproduction to someone who has spent time with the original.

This is not a failure of craft. The compound is well-built and entirely functional. It is simply the building of people who did not grow up knowing what a great house compound was and had to learn it from observation rather than inheritance. The stone will weather eventually. In a generation or two the compound will look like it has always been there.

It does not yet look like it has always been there.

## THE NUMBERED AVENUES

A note on how the city names its own streets, since visitors are forever getting lost in it.

Antitheis is built in rings, and most of its streets have grown the way streets grow anywhere: along the contours of the ground, around the hills the city never troubled to level, named for whatever trade or family or accident of history happened to settle them. The lane behind the Worn Lintel, Tailor's Bridge, the Cattle Steps. These names are organic, local, and frequently contradictory; two streets a quarter-mile apart may carry the same name, and no one minds. The city is not a wheel of clean lines but a thing that has followed its own ground, swelling and kinking around its hills as it pleased.

Against this confusion stand the eight Avenues. These are the only formally numbered streets in Antitheis, laid straight as spokes from the outer ring through the Mid City into the inner ward, and they are numbered like the hours of a clock face. The Eighth runs due north; from it the count goes round clockwise — the First pointing north-east, the Second east, and so on — so that the Seventh, last before the circle closes again, points north-west. Where an avenue passes through each ring it takes the ring's name: a single spoke is Outer Seventh Avenue out at the wall and Inner Seventh Avenue as it nears the great houses. Only the odd-numbered avenues — the Seventh, the Fifth, the Third, the First — widen into a square where the Mid City crosses them; the Seventh's is Seventh Square, and the even avenues have no such thing. The citizens, who have no patience for the full forms, simply say "the Seventh," or arrange to meet "on the seven-eight," meaning the wedge of city caught between two of the spokes.

It is a foreigner's first lesson and his most useful one: if a street carries a number, it will take you to the centre. If it carries a name, it will take you somewhere only the locals understand.

---

## THE OUTSKIRTS

A city the size of Antitheis does not end at its wall; it bleeds outward, and the country it bleeds into is neither city nor true countryside but something between the two — a wide, untidy belt that thickens against the gates and thins, by slow degrees, into farmland and dark.

Hard against the wall the belt is all commerce, and commerce of a particular character. Here the farmers of the hinterland meet the city's merchants to deal in bulk — grain, hides, timber, beasts — at the Gate Markets, as everyone calls them: half-permanent and half not, raised and struck with the seasons and the weight of the traffic. Threaded through them, and thickest along the approach to each gate, is a second and smaller trade that feeds on the people waiting to get in: stalls of water and bread and charms against the road, scribes and sellers of papers and stamps for those whose documents the watch will not like, and men who will, for a coin, stand in the gate-queue in your place through a long hot day. Not all of it is honest. A good deal of it is the opposite. The nearer the gate, the higher the prices climb and the thinner the charms' promises wear, for a traveller arriving footsore and anxious is precisely the customer this trade was built for.

Further out the noise falls away. The markets give way to farmsteads and ranches, to hedged fields and long quiet lanes and the slow tempo of village life the city has not quite reached. And set among them, at intervals, ringing the capital like beads strung on a cord, stand the forts and garrison-camps — squat, unlovely, and deliberately placed — by which Antitheis reminds its own hinterland, and anyone walking up to it, exactly whose country this is.
