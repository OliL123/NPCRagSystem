# THE KEPT FIRE
### *Notes on an Old Faith of the Common Hearth*
*Krad Kraps*

---

*Of all the things I have watched this Empire try to grind out of its people, this is the one I have come to love best, and the one I am most careful how I set down, because to describe it too well is to hand its enemies a lantern. So I will describe it a little poorly, on purpose, and trust the reader to understand why.*

*There is a faith kept across Ath that has no temple, no priest, no name for its god, and no god left alive to hear it. I have found it at an inn on the Carvallen road and in a back room of the Antitheis Ring, kept by people who had never met and would not have used the same word for what they were doing, if they used a word at all. It is the oldest thing I have found on this continent, and the quietest, and I have come to suspect it will outlast everything raised to replace it, for the plain reason that no one can find it in order to burn it.*

---

## THE GOD THAT DIED

The faith remembers a small god. Not one of the great powers the Empire argues about, but a minor household thing, the sort every hearth once kept: a god of the fire and the field, of the grain got in before the frost and the warm room on the far side of a hard winter. That much any farmer could tell you. What took me longer to see is that under the farming and the fire the god was really about something else, and the something else was *belonging*. It was the god of the moment a stranger is given a seat by the fire and a bowl of what is cooking and, an hour later, is no longer quite a stranger. Kinship made rather than born. The old keepers understood, better than our philosophers do, that a family is not only a matter of blood. It is a matter of shared warmth and shared food, repeated until the sharing becomes the bond.

That god is dead. I want to be plain about it, because the people who keep the faith are plain about it, in their way. When its last true believers died in the hard years, the god died with them, the way such small gods do when there is no one left to hold them up. What is kept now is kept for a dead thing whose very name has gone. There is a notion among the keepers that a person dies twice, once when the body fails and once, later, at the last time their name is ever spoken aloud. By that reckoning the god is halfway into the second death, and the faith is nothing more, and nothing less, than the act of not letting go of the rope. I have spent my life mourning what the Fall erased. I did not expect to find, in a farm kitchen, a whole religion made of exactly that grief, practised as a chore.

## THE KEPT FIRE

The daily devotion is a fire that is never allowed to die. Each night the coals are banked, buried in their own ash so they smoulder low until morning, and each dawn they are coaxed back to flame. A well-tended house-fire under this faith is not lit and let out; it is *continuous*, carried unbroken across years, sometimes across a life. The beauty of the practice, and its cunning, is that it is indistinguishable from ordinary good housekeeping. A woman banking her coals against the cold looks like nothing at all. She is, in fact, at prayer, and there is no way for any watchman living to tell the difference.

## THE WINTER FIRE

In the deep of winter the fire takes on a second purpose. Through the longest, coldest nights the keepers hold extra flame, a candle in a window, the hearth kept high, and they will tell you, if they tell you anything, that it is to guide the dead home. Not the dead in general. The lost ones, the ones who died far from any warmth, so that their spirits, wandering the cold, might see a lit window and come in out of the dark and find respite at a hearth instead of freezing forever in the black between houses. It is a hospitality that does not stop at death. The same instinct that seats the living stranger by the fire will not leave even a dead stranger out in the cold.

## THE SPRING BREAD

At the first day of spring the keepers bake a bread, and shape it, small, into the figure of a woman or a child, and eat it. It is a mourning and a welcome at once: a grieving for those the winter took, and a taking-in of the new life the turning year brings. The rite is plainly a surviving scrap of some larger lost feast, most of which is gone past recovering. What ties it to the winter fire is a detail I found quietly astonishing. The ash from the winter dead-guiding fires is kept through the cold months and worked into the dough of the spring bread. Death feeds the hearth, the hearth yields ash, the ash goes into the bread, the bread is the new life. The whole year closes in a single pair of hands, and opens again. I have rarely seen a theology so complete carried by people who would laugh to hear it called one.

## THE EMPTY PLACE

In a house that keeps the old ways there is a place set for the dead. An extra bowl at the hearth, or bread and salt left in the warmth overnight, or a stool that no one sits on. It is kept for the household's own dead, who are held to return to the warm room they knew. What moved me most, when I finally understood it, is what has happened to that empty place over the long years. The god of this faith was the great host, the one who set a seat for every stranger and every returning dead. Now there is no one left to set a seat for *it*. So the keepers' empty place, without their quite intending it, has come to hold the nameless god as well, the last guest, the host become the kept. The people who spent the faith's whole history making room for others now, unknowing, make room for the god that taught them how.

## THE SHRINE YOU CANNOT FIND

This faith has no idol, and that absence is the heart of it. Where another shrine would set an image, the hearth-faith sets nothing, or rather it sets an absence on purpose: a covered bowl, a smooth stone worked into the chimney-breast, a mark scratched where the firelight happens to fall, a dish of grain or salt or the kept ash. The god has no name and no face, so it is worshipped as a shape of emptiness at the warm center of a home. A woven figure of the last sheaf of the harvest may be kept through the winter and burned or buried come spring, as the bread-woman takes its place. Offerings are set on the windowsill overnight, the sill being the threshold the dead can reach. And there are always the preserved things, the salted and dried and pickled stores that are winter survival made keepable, some portion of which is set aside and never sold, because that portion belongs to the god.

I hope the reader now sees why this faith cannot be stamped out, though the present order would dearly like to. There is nothing to raid. The shrine is the hearth, the liturgy is the housework, the priest is whoever is keeping the house. You cannot outlaw a woman for banking her own fire or leaving bread on her own sill. The faith survives precisely because it refused, long ago, to look like a faith at all.

## THE HEARTH AND THE SICK

There is one more office of the hearth I must record, because it is the one the keepers themselves value most and speak of least. The hearth is where the sick are tended. The old remedies belong to it, the brews and poultices and steamings made at the fire, with certain words said low over them while they are made. I am a documenter of customs and no physician, and I will report only what I was told and what I saw, which is this: those who keep the old ways will swear to you that the hearth-remedies mend a shade faster and surer than the very same herbs bought from a market physic, and that a fever tended with the words breaks a night sooner than one tended without. I record it as they believe it. I will note only, and then leave it, that I heard this from too many people, in too many places that had never spoken to one another, for me to be entirely comfortable filing it under mere superstition and walking on. The Collegium has a department that would find the question interesting, and a great many reasons never to ask it aloud.

## WHAT IT COSTS

The Empire, and House Bituris that speaks for it, names all of this heresy. The Gospel of Man has no room for a dead god of the fire, and less room for the possibility that keeping faith with it might do anything at all. Those who are exposed keeping the old ways lose everything, and those who cannot flee are taken, and I have written elsewhere and will not repeat here about where the taken go. So the faith has gone the only place it can go, which is further into plain sight, and further underground, both at once. In the country it hides inside housework. In the city it has gone below, into rooms with no windows, kept by people the light is no longer safe for, who tend a fire in the dark for the dead who are lost, which underground is nearer the truth than they can afford to say.

---

*I keep no faith of my own; I have read too much history to trust an institution with my soul. But I have sat at these hearths, and been made, briefly and against all my professional distance, to feel like kin, by people who had nothing and set a place for me anyway. If there is a holier act on this continent I have not found it, and I have looked. The god is dead and its name is gone and still, on the coldest nights, the fires are kept, so that no one, living or dead, has to be out in the cold alone. Whatever that is, it is not nothing. It may be the last un-erased thing.*

*— K.K.*
